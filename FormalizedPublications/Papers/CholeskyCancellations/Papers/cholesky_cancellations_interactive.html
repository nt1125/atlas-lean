Accidental Cancellations in Cholesky Factorization

Nam Trang and Meta AI

Reviewed by Paul Bendich and Patrick Tam

PDF: cholesky_cancellations.pdf.

Lean library: nt1125/atlas-lean.

Abstract.

Sparse Cholesky factorization allocates storage from the symbolic fill pattern \(G^+\) alone, assuming that no position predicted nonzero ever cancels. Over-allocation never threatens correctness, so the assumption is safe in practice, but it leaves open an existential question, recorded by the first author in unpublished notes circulated in 2021 as two conjectures. Call an ordering \(\sigma\) clean for an SPD matrix \(A\) if every position predicted nonzero in \(G^+_\sigma\) is nonzero in the factor \(L\). Conjecture 1: every irreducible SPD matrix admits a clean ordering. Conjecture 2: every irreducible SPD matrix admits an ordering whose first Schur complement has no cancellation at a predicted-nonzero position.

We refute both. Conjecture 1 fails at every even order \(n\ge6\), via an explicit family \(M_{2m}\) on the complete graph. The family is not exhibited but derived: classifying the zero-configurations available at a level with four surviving vertices forces a fixed-point-free involution — hence even order — forces the magnitudes \(AB\) within pairs and blocks \([[A,B],[B,A]]\) across them, and forces the parameters onto the measure-zero curve \(A^2+(1-B^2)A-1=0\). Conversely every point of that curve yields a counterexample at every even order, so the construction is a one-parameter family rather than a single matrix. Conjecture 2 already fails at order \(4\), via \(A^*\) with spectrum \(\{9,3,3,1\}\): none of its \(24\) orderings has a cancellation-free first Schur complement, yet \(8\) of them give a clean factor — the refill phenomenon that separates the two conjectures, and the trap that invalidates any argument conflating Schur-complement zeros with factor zeros.

Positively, Conjecture 1 holds for every irreducible SPD matrix of order at most \(4\), by an elementary argument; order five we leave open, and we settle no further order exhaustively. At order six the complete graph \(K_6\) fails, by the family above. Forests are clean in the strong form that every ordering is clean — recovering, in factorization language, a covariance-faithfulness theorem of Malouche and Rajaratnam — and so are \(2\)-path strips and dominating-vertex fans, chordal graphs with exactly one triangle (adaptively; no fixed ordering suffices), and chordal graphs of clique number at most \(3\) with pairwise edge-disjoint triangles. Odd orders \(n\ge7\) remain open, and we explain why the pairing argument that settles the even orders does not extend to them.

1. Introduction

1.1. Sparse Cholesky and the price of fill

Let \(A\) be a real symmetric positive definite matrix of order \(n\). Its Cholesky factorization \(A = LL^\top\) is the workhorse of direct methods for the linear systems arising in finite-element analysis, interior-point methods, Gaussian process regression, and maximum-likelihood estimation in graphical models. When \(A\) is sparse, the cost of the factorization is governed not by \(n\) but by the number of nonzeros in \(L\), and that number depends on the order in which variables are eliminated. Eliminating a variable makes its surviving neighbors mutually adjacent, so positions that were structurally zero in \(A\) may become nonzero in \(L\). These are the fill entries, and controlling them is the central problem of sparse direct methods.

The combinatorial description is due to Rose, Tarjan and Lueker [RTL76]. Represent the sparsity of \(A\) by its pattern graph \(G_A\), with an edge \(\{i,j\}\) whenever \(A_{ij} \neq 0\). For an ordering (or permutation) \(\sigma\) of \(\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}\), play the elimination game: repeatedly delete the next vertex (in the ordering given by \(\sigma\)) and complete its neighborhood into a clique. The resulting graph \(G^+_\sigma\) contains every position that can be nonzero in \(L\), and the fill-path theorem characterizes it exactly: \(\{j,k\}\) is an edge of \(G^+_\sigma\) precisely when \(G_A\) contains a \(j\)–\(k\) path all of whose interior vertices precede both endpoints. Choosing \(\sigma\) to minimize fill is NP-hard [Yan81], which is why practical codes rely on heuristics and on the elimination-tree machinery surveyed by Heath, Ng and Peyton [HNP91] and Davis [Dav06].

1.2. The no-cancellation assumption

Every sparse Cholesky implementation performs a symbolic factorization before touching a single floating-point number: it computes \(G^+_\sigma\) from \(G_A\) alone and allocates exactly that much storage. This step is purely graph-theoretic, and it is where an assumption enters. The code takes for granted that a position predicted nonzero is nonzero – that once an entry fills in, it never cancels back to zero. Demmel's lecture notes state it without apology: “assume that there is no cancellation, i.e. that once an entry of \(A\) gets filled in, it never cancels out later (which would be highly unlikely anyway)” [Dem24].

The assumption is harmless for its intended purpose, and it is worth being precise about why. Symbolic factorization produces an upper bound on the structure of \(L\). If a predicted nonzero happens to cancel, the code has reserved a slot it does not need: it over-allocates slightly and computes the correct answer. Cancellation is a performance question, never a correctness question. Nor is the parenthetical wrong: a cancellation requires an exact algebraic coincidence among the entries of \(A\), and such coincidences form a set of Lebesgue measure zero.

So the practice is sound and the heuristic is true. But both are statements about a fixed ordering and about typical matrices, and neither answers the question a mathematician would ask next.

In sum, the results in this paper do not contradict expert expectations, they live on its complement. Experts expected the generic statement, and it holds; the conjectures are existential and quantify over all SPD matrices, including the measure-zero locus where the counterexamples lie.

1.3. The existential question

Fix a matrix, not an ordering, and ask whether some ordering is free of cancellation. Call \(\sigma\) clean for \(A\) if for every edge \(\{j,k\}\in G^+_\sigma\), letting \(S\) be the set of vertices preceding \(\min(\sigma^{-1}(j),\sigma^{-1}(k))\) in \(\sigma\), we have \((A/A_S)_{jk}\neq0\) — i.e. the entry is nonzero at the stage where the earlier of \(j,k\) is pivoted, so the predicted nonzero is realized in \(L\). This is the factor definition, not the Schur-complement definition requiring \((A/A_S)_{jk}\neq0\) for every stage \(S\) whenever \(\{j,k\}\in G_S\); the latter conflates Schur zeros with factor cancellations and would make \(A^*\) have zero clean orderings, contradicting Theorem 5.1 and the refill phenomenon. Two conjectures, recorded by Trang in unpublished notes in 2021, ask:

Conjecture 1. Every irreducible SPD matrix admits a clean ordering.

Conjecture 2. Every irreducible SPD matrix admits an ordering whose first Schur complement has no cancellation at a predicted-nonzero position.

These are genuinely different from the implementation's assumption, in two ways. First, they are existential: they permit the ordering to depend on the numerical entries of \(A\), whereas symbolic factorization must choose \(\sigma\) from \(G_A\) alone. Second, they quantify over all SPD matrices, including the measure-zero ones the genericity heuristic sets aside. A counterexample must therefore be an exact algebraic object, and this is exactly why “cancellation is unlikely” and “Conjecture 1 is false” can both be true: the two statements live on complementary sets.

Conjecture 2 is the stronger demand, and the gap between them is the phenomenon we call refill. A zero created in one Schur complement is not yet a cancellation in \(L\): the entry \((j,k)\) is committed only when the earlier of \(j,k\) is pivoted, and an intervening elimination can restore it to a nonzero value. Any analysis that conflates Schur-complement zeros with factor zeros will reach false conclusions.

1.4. The same question in statistics

The existential question has a second life in Gaussian graphical models, where a zero in a Schur complement is a conditional independence: \((A/A_S)_{jk} = 0\) says exactly that \(X_j\) and \(X_k\) are conditionally independent given \(X_S\). A distribution is faithful to its graph when no conditional independence holds beyond those the graph implies, so a cancellation at a predicted-nonzero position is a failure of faithfulness.

That literature has settled one case decisively. Malouche and Rajaratnam [MR11] proved, using the covariance–precision path formula of Jones and West [JW05], that a Gaussian whose covariance graph is a tree is necessarily covariance faithful. The dual statement for concentration graphs is older still: Becker, Geiger and Meek [BGM05] proved that a Gaussian whose concentration graph is a tree is necessarily concentration faithful. In our language the covariance version is Conjecture 1 for forests, in the strong form that every ordering is clean – proved fifteen years ago under a different name, its dual a decade earlier again, and neither cited in the numerical literature that assumes it. Beyond trees the two communities part company: Meek [Mee95] and Uhler, Raskutti, B"uhlmann and Yu [URBY13] study when faithfulness holds generically, showing that unfaithful distributions have measure zero while strong-faithfulness failures occupy a set of positive measure, but neither literature asks whether some ordering can always be chosen to avoid the failures that do occur.

1.5. Results

We answer the existential question negatively in general and positively on a substantial and now precisely delimited class.

Conjecture 2 is false at order 4 (Theorem 5.1): an explicit matrix \(A^*\) with spectrum \(\{9,3,3,1\}\) has no ordering whose first Schur complement is cancellation-free, although \(8\) of its \(24\) orderings give a clean factor – the refill phenomenon in its simplest form.

Conjecture 1 holds for \(n\le4\) by an elementary case analysis of the six connected patterns on four vertices, with no computation (Theorem 5.2), and is false at every even order at least 6 (Theorem 5.8), via a family \(M_{2m}\) built from a pairing of the vertices, with within-pair entries of magnitude \(AB\) and cross-pair blocks \([[A,B],[B,A]]\). The family is not exhibited but derived: at any level with four surviving vertices, exactly three of the \(4096=2^{12}\) candidate zero-configurations cover all four pivots, and each is a fixed-point-free involution (Lemma 5.5). The three magnitudes and the block structure are forced as the unique solution of the resulting additive system; the requirement of a fixed-point-free involution is precisely why the order must be even. The parameters are forced onto the measure-zero curve \(A^2+(1-B^2)A-1=0\), and conversely every point of that curve with \(0<B<1\) yields a counterexample (Remark 5.9), so the construction is a one-parameter family; the instance \(B=1/2\), with \(A=(-3+\sqrt{73})/8\), is the one we verify explicitly.

Order 5 remains open (Section 7). At order six the complete graph \(K_6\) fails, by Theorem 5.8, and the single \(14\)-edge pattern \(K_6-e\) remains open.

Structural classes. Independently of the order-by-order picture we prove Conjecture 1 for forests in the strong form above; for 2-path strips and dominating-vertex fans; for chordal graphs with exactly one triangle, adaptively, with an explicit demonstration that no fixed ordering suffices; and for chordal graphs of clique number at most \(3\) whose triangles are pairwise edge-disjoint.

The counterexamples refuting both conjectures, and the structural classes of Section 4 beyond forests, were found with AI assistance and insight; AI finds the counterexamples in Section 5, provides the calculations showing the counterexamples work, and comes up with key ideas for the proofs in Section 4.

Odd orders at least 7 remain open. The reason is structural rather than computational: the classification of Lemma 5.5 that forces the even construction requires exactly four surviving vertices, and its injectivity step fails at five, so parity confers no protection at odd orders. Remark 5.7 records this together with the two computations that delimit the position — no scheme leaving one survivor at every vertex exists at order five, and exhaustive search at order seven is infeasible.

1.6. Method

Three devices carry the analysis. We recast cleanliness as a covering problem: a slot \((S, \{j,k\})\) asserts \((A/A_S)_{jk} = 0\), and Conjecture 1 fails exactly when some blocking family of slots – one hitting every ordering – is realizable by an SPD matrix. Second, since a cancellation reads \(r_{jk} = r_{uj}r_{uk}\), the substitution \(t^{(S)}_{jk} = -\log|r^{(S)}_{jk}|\) makes every cancellation additive, \(t^{(S)}_{jk} = t^{(S)}_{uj} + t^{(S)}_{uk}\), with \(t^{(S)}_{jk}>0\) forced by strict definiteness; obstructions then become statements about a linear system, refutable by Stiemke's lemma [Sti15, Gor73] with exact rational certificates. Third, unrealizability is monotone in the slot family (Lemma 3.18), which is what makes exhaustive search over patterns finite and sound.

1.7. Related work

Four strands of literature bear on the existential question, and it is worth saying what we take from each.

Sparse direct methods. The combinatorial model is due to Rose, Tarjan and Lueker [RTL76]: the elimination game, the fill graph \(G^+_\sigma\), and the fill-path theorem characterizing its edges by paths whose interior has already been eliminated. Everything symbolic in this paper is a consequence of that characterization, recorded here as Proposition 2.10; what we add is the question of when the symbolic prediction is numerically exact. Minimizing fill is NP-hard [Yan81], which is why production codes choose the ordering by heuristics, and the accounts of Heath, Ng and Peyton [HNP91] and of Davis [Dav06] describe the two-phase practice that Section 6 addresses: a symbolic phase allocates for \(G^+_\sigma\), and a numeric phase fills that structure in. Demmel's lecture notes [Dem24] state the underlying assumption explicitly. Our results separate two readings of it: as an allocation bound it is correct, since the numeric pattern is always contained in the symbolic one (Definition 2.12), but as an existential statement about orderings it is false from order six onwards.

Chordal graphs. The positive results of Section 4 proceed by choosing simplicial pivots, so they rest on the classical theory: Dirac's theorem that a chordal graph has a simplicial vertex [Dir61], the characterization of chordality by perfect elimination orderings [FG65], and the standard treatment of Golumbic [Gol80]. Our setting differs from the usual one in a way worth emphasizing. A perfect elimination ordering is optimal for fill, but optimality for fill is not cleanliness: Remark 4.6 exhibits a \(2\)-path strip and SPD realizations of it for which no fill-free ordering is clean, so the induction of Theorem 4.8 must sometimes create fill deliberately. The strip and fan classes are partial \(2\)-trees in the sense of Bodlaender [Bod98], and general partial \(2\)-trees are the natural next case, which is why they head the list of open problems in Section 7.

Gaussian graphical models and faithfulness. Under the dictionary of Lauritzen [Lau96], a Schur-complement zero is a conditional independence, so a cancellation at a predicted-nonzero position is a failure of faithfulness. Malouche and Rajaratnam [MR11] proved that a Gaussian whose covariance graph is a tree is covariance faithful; in our language that is the Fill-Graph Conjecture for forests in the strong form that every ordering is clean, and Theorem 4.1 re-derives it in factorization language using the path formula of Jones and West [JW05], which is also the identity that drives the proof. Trek separation [STD10] gives the general combinatorial criterion for vanishing minors in such models, and our slots are instances of the vanishing conditions it classifies. The faithfulness literature then parts company with our question, and the difference is worth stating precisely: Meek [Mee95], Zhang and Spirtes [ZS08], and Uhler, Raskutti, B"uhlmann and Yu [URBY13] ask when faithfulness holds generically or strongly, and show that unfaithful distributions form a set of measure zero while strong-faithfulness failures occupy a set of positive measure. Those are statements about typical matrices. Ours is existential and quantifies over all SPD matrices, so a counterexample must be an exact algebraic object and necessarily lives on the measure-zero locus that this literature sets aside; Appendix F explains how the two coexist.

Tools. Three further bodies of work are used technically rather than thematically. The quotient identity for Schur complements, which is what licenses treating a level as a set rather than a sequence, is due to Haynsworth and to Crabtree and Haynsworth [Hay68, CH69]; it appears here as Lemma 2.4. Which zero patterns are realizable by an SPD matrix at all is the positive-definite completion problem of Grone, Johnson, S'a and Wolkowicz [GJSW84], with clique trees [BP93] as the structure behind the chordal case; this is the background against which our notion of realizability should be read, since a blocking family refutes the conjecture only if some SPD matrix realizes it. The conditional-independence axioms for Gaussians catalogued by Lnenicka and Matus [LM07] contain our Nesting Lemma 3.14 as the weak-transitivity axiom. Finally, the infeasibility certificates of Section 3 are an application of Stiemke's transposition theorem [Sti15], a refinement of Gordan's [Gor73].

1.9. Why it matters

These results close a foundational gap between theory and practice felt in several communities. For numerical linear algebra and sparse direct solvers, symbolic factorization allocates \(G^+\) from the graph alone assuming no cancellation; we show the assumption is safe for correctness (over-allocation) yet false existentially: \(G^L_\sigma=G^+_\sigma\) cannot be guaranteed for all SPD matrices, the first failure occurring at order \(6\) (\(K_6\), via \(M_6\)). Order \(4\) already obstructs the weaker first-Schur version (\(A^*\): \(0/24\) stage-\(1\) clean), while its \(8/24\) clean factors show that refill separates the two questions.

For graph theory and combinatorial matrix theory, we map the boundary of when a chordal completion can be realized without algebraic accident: forests are strongly clean (every ordering clean), \(2\)-path strips, fans, one-triangle adaptive, and edge-disjoint triangles with \(\omega\le3\) are clean, while \(K_6\) fails. The failure requires near-completeness rather than density or clique number alone, and the \(4\)-vertex pairing involution forced on the curve \(A^2+(1-B^2)A-1=0\) explains why.

For statistics, graphical models, and causal inference, a cancellation \((A/A_S)_{jk}=0\) is a failure of faithfulness. We re-derive, in factorization language, Malouche and Rajaratnam's result that forests are faithful in the strong form every ordering clean (proved under a different name fifteen years ago, never cited in the numerical literature that assumes it). We exhibit measure-zero unfaithful \(M_{2m}\) on \(K_{2m}\) with \(A^2+(1-B^2)A-1=0\), and explain why unfaithfulness has measure zero (faithfulness is generic) yet coexists with existential failure.

For theoretical computer science, the covering problem over slots and the integer Stiemke certificates \(y^\top T\ge0\) used to refute blocking families provide a transposition-theorem view of when a chordal completion can be realized without algebraic accident.

2. Setting, vocabulary, and conjectures

This section fixes every notion used in Sections 3–5. It is meant to be read once and consulted thereafter; Table 1 collects the notation. Three points are worth flagging before the formal definitions. First, we work with two graphs at once: a symbolic graph predicted from the sparsity pattern alone, and the numeric pattern of the actual Schur complement. The whole subject is the gap between them. Second, elimination is indexed by sets rather than sequences, which is legitimate by the quotient property (Lemma 2.4). Third, a slot is a formal assertion that some entry vanishes; it becomes true or false only relative to a matrix, and that distinction drives the entire method.

2.1. SPD matrices and Schur complements of sets

Definition 2.1 (SPD).

A symmetric \(n\times n\) real matrix \(A\) is symmetric positive definite (SPD), written \(A\succ0\), if \(x^\top Ax>0\) for every \(x\neq0\). Throughout, \(V=\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}\) indexes the rows and columns of \(A\), and we freely call the elements of \(V\) vertices.

Definition 2.2 (Schur complement of a set).

Let \(A\) be symmetric and let \(S\subseteq V\) be any subset with \(A_{SS}\succ0\); put \(R=V\setminus S\) and write \(A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{SS}&A_{SR}\\A_{RS}&A_{RR}\end{pmatrix}\) after a symmetric permutation. The Schur complement of \(S\) in \(A\) is the matrix on \(R\)

\[A/A_S = A_{RR}-A_{RS}(A_{SS})^{-1}A_{SR}.\]

For a single vertex, \(A/A_{\{v\}}=B-rr^\top/a\) when \(A=\begin{pmatrix}a&r^\top\\r&B\end{pmatrix}\) with \(a=A_{vv}\). For an ordering \(\sigma=(v_1,\dots,v_n)\) we abbreviate \(S^{(0)}=A\) and \(S^{(j)}=A/A_{\{v_1,\dots,v_j\}}\).

Lemma 2.4 (Quotient property, transitivity of Schur complements).

Let \(A\succ0\), \(S\subset V\) and \(v\in V\setminus S\), and set \(M=A/A_S\). Then

\[M/M_{\{v\}} = A/A_{S\cup\{v\}}\]

as matrices on \(V\setminus(S\cup\{v\})\). That is, eliminating \(S\) and then \(v\) gives the same result as eliminating \(S\cup\{v\}\) at once.

Remark 2.5 (Levels and stages).

Because of Proposition 2.3 and Lemma 2.4 we may speak of a level: a set \(S\subseteq V\) of already-eliminated vertices, with \(A/A_S\) the matrix currently being factored and \(R=V\setminus S\) the survivors. The stage is the number \(|S|\) of completed eliminations, so an ordering passes through levels \(\emptyset=S_0\subset S_1\subset\cdots\subset S_n=V\) with \(|S_j|=j\). “Level” is thus a set and “stage” is a counter; a statement “at level \(S\)” is a statement about the single matrix \(A/A_S\), whereas a statement “at stage \(j\)” refers to whichever level of size \(j\) the ordering has reached.

2.2. Pivots, the elimination game, and fill graphs

Definition 2.6 (Pivot).

Let \(S\) be a level and \(p\in R=V\setminus S\) a survivor, and put \(M=A/A_S\). To pivot at \(p\) is to pass from level \(S\) to level \(S\cup\{p\}\), that is, to replace \(M\) by

\[M/M_{\{p\}},\qquad (M/M_{\{p\}})_{jk}=M_{jk}-\frac{M_{jp}M_{kp}}{M_{pp}} \quad (j,k\in R\setminus\{p\}),\]

a symmetric rank-one update of the surviving block. We call \(D=M_{pp}>0\) the pivot value, the vector \((M_{kp})_{k\in R\setminus\{p\}}\) the pivot column, and the two factors \(M_{jp}\), \(M_{kp}\) appearing in the update of the pair \((j,k)\) the legs of that update (the legs of the route \(j-p-k\)). An ordering \(\sigma\) is thus exactly a schedule of \(n\) pivots, and the Cholesky factor is assembled from the pivot columns (Definition 2.23).

Definition pivotEntry

Definition 2.7 (Pattern graph, elimination game, \(G_S\)).

The pattern graph \(G_A\) has vertex set \(V\) and an edge \(\{i,j\}\), \(i\neq j\), exactly when \(A_{ij}\neq0\). The elimination game on \(G_A\) is the following purely combinatorial process. Fix an ordering \(\sigma=(v_1,\dots,v_n)\) of \(V\) and put \(H_0=G_A\). At step \(i\) let

\[F_i=\bigl\{\{x,y\}: x\neq y,\ x,y\in N_{H_{i-1}}(v_i),\ \{x,y\}\notin E(H_{i-1})\bigr\}\]

be the fill edges created by \(v_i\), and let \(H_i\) be \(H_{i-1}\) together with \(F_i\), restricted to the surviving vertices \(\{v_{i+1},\dots,v_n\}\). Neighborhoods are always taken in the current graph \(H_{i-1}\), not in \(G_A\); this is what makes fill beget fill.

For a level \(S\subseteq V\) we define \(G_S\), a graph on the survivors \(V\setminus S\), as follows: run the game along any ordering of \(V\) whose first \(|S|\) entries enumerate \(S\), and stop after \(|S|\) steps. Thus \(G_S=H_{|S|}\) for such an ordering, and in particular

\[G_\emptyset=H_0=G_A,\qquad G_{\{v_1,\dots,v_i\}}=H_i .\]

Remark 2.8.

Definition 2.9 (Fill graph \(G^+_\sigma\)).

original edges together with every fill edge ever created, and no edge or vertex is ever removed. Definition 2.14 (Graph notions used throughout).

Let \(G\) be a finite simple graph. \(\omega(G)\) is its clique number, the size of a largest clique. A vertex \(v\) is simplicial if \(N(v)\) is a clique, and pendant if \(|N(v)|=1\). \(G\) is chordal if it has no induced cycle of length at least \(4\). A cut vertex is a vertex whose removal disconnects the component containing it, a block is a maximal connected subgraph without a cut vertex, and the bipartite block-cut tree is the tree whose nodes are the blocks and the cut vertices, with a block adjacent to each cut vertex it contains; its leaves are always blocks. \(G\) is a clique sum of \(G_1\) and \(G_2\) along \(K\) if \(G=G_1\cup G_2\) and \(G_1\cap G_2=K\) is a clique.

2.4. Cancellations, apexes, and what a pivot can destroy

Definition 2.19 (Cancellation graph, zeroing, apex, covering, surviving).

Let \(S\) be a level and \(p\in V\setminus S\) a survivor. Put

\[C_p(S)=\bigl\{\{j,k\}: j,k\in V\setminus(S\cup\{p\}),\ j\neq k,\ (A/A_S)_{pp}(A/A_S)_{jk}=(A/A_S)_{pj}(A/A_S)_{pk}\bigr\},\]

the cancellation graph of \(p\) at level \(S\), and write \(C_p:=C_p(\emptyset)\). Thus \(C_p(S)\) is exactly the set of pairs that the pivot at \(p\) destroys: by Definition 2.6,

\[\{j,k\}\in C_p(S)\iff (A/A_{S\cup\{p\}})_{jk}=0 ,\]

and we then say that \(p\) zeroes the pair \(\{j,k\}\) at level \(S\), that \(p\) is the apex of that cancellation, and that \(j-p-k\) is the route. We say \(C_p(S)\) covers a vertex \(q\) if some pair of \(C_p(S)\) contains \(q\), and that \(q\) survives the pivot at \(p\) otherwise; equivalently, \(q\) survives if \((A/A_{S\cup\{p\}})_{qk}\neq0\) for every \(k\in V\setminus(S\cup\{p,q\})\). A survivor \(p\) is a clean pivot at level \(S\) if \(C_p(S)\cap E(G_S)=\emptyset\), i.e. it destroys no predicted-nonzero pair; for a triangle \(T\) we say \(p\) is a clean apex for \(T\) if \(C_p(S)\) contains no pair of \(T\). The unqualified phrase clean apex means \(C_p(S)=\emptyset\).

Definition 2.23 (Factor entry).

Let \(\sigma=(v_1,\dots,v_n)\), \(S_{j-1}=\{v_1,\dots,v_{j-1}\}\), \(M=S^{(j-1)}=A/A_{S_{j-1}}\), \(p=v_j\) and \(D_j=M_{pp}\). Then for every \(k\) after \(p\) in \(\sigma\),

\[L_{kp}=M_{kp}/\sqrt{D_j}=(A/A_{S_{j-1}})_{kp}/\sqrt{D_j},\]

so the \(p\)-th column of \(L\) is the scaled pivot column and \(L_{kp}\neq0\) iff \((A/A_{S_{j-1}})_{kp}\neq0\). Consequently, if \(j\) precedes \(k\) and \(S\) is the set of vertices preceding \(j\), then \(L_{kj}\neq0\) iff \((A/A_S)_{jk}\neq0\): the pair \(\{j,k\}\) is committed to \(L\) exactly when the earlier of \(j,k\) is pivoted, and \(G^L_\sigma\) is the pattern of \(L+L^\top\).

Definition 2.24 (Clean ordering).

An ordering \(\sigma\) is clean for \(A\) if for every \(\{j,k\}\in G^+_\sigma\), writing \(S=\{u:\sigma^{-1}(u)<\min(\sigma^{-1}(j),\sigma^{-1}(k))\}\), we have \((A/A_S)_{jk}\neq0\). Definition 2.25 (Strategies: fixed, adaptive, peeling).

A fixed ordering for a pattern \(G\) is a single permutation \(\sigma\), chosen from \(G\) alone, to be used for every SPD matrix with \(G_A=G\). An adaptive strategy chooses each pivot as a function of the current matrix \(A/A_S\); it therefore produces, for each SPD \(A\) with \(G_A=G\), an ordering \(\sigma(A)\). We say \(G\) admits an adaptive clean ordering if some adaptive strategy yields a clean ordering for every SPD \(A\) with \(G_A=G\). Theorem 4.8(ii) shows the distinction is real: there are patterns admitting an adaptive clean ordering for which no fixed \(\sigma\) is clean for all matrices. To peel a simplicial survivor \(v\) is to pivot at it; since \(N(v)\) is a clique, no fill is created, and if \(v\) is pendant then no pair of survivors is updated at all, so peeling a pendant vertex can neither create nor destroy a zero. The two named moves END and SECOND used for strips in Section 4 are, respectively, peeling the end vertex of a strip and pivoting its neighbor; they are defined precisely in Definition 4.5.

2.6. Slots, blocking families, and realizability

Definition 2.28 (Slot; active).

A slot is a formal triple \((S,j,k)\) with \(S\subseteq V\) a level and \(j,k\in V\setminus S\), \(j\neq k\). It is a demand, not a fact: it asserts the equation \((A/A_S)_{jk}=0\). Given a particular matrix \(A\), the slot is active for \(A\) if that equation actually holds. Thus slots are combinatorial objects quantified over, and activity is the only notion that refers to numbers; a family of slots is a system of equations whose solvability is the question.

Definition 2.29 (Spoiling, blocking, realizable, feasible).

A slot \((S,j,k)\) spoils an ordering \(\sigma\) if \(S\) is exactly the set of predecessors of the earlier of \(j,k\) in \(\sigma\) and \(\{j,k\}\in G_S\); that is, the position is predicted nonzero and is committed to \(L\) precisely at level \(S\), so activity of the slot makes \(\sigma\) unclean. A family \(H\) of slots is blocking for a pattern \(G\) if every permutation of \(V\) is spoiled by some slot of \(H\). \(H\) is realizable for \(G\) if there is an SPD \(A\) with \(G_A=G\) for which every slot of \(H\) is active. A family of slots at a single fixed level \(S\) is feasible if the additive system \(Tt^{(S)}=0\) of Definition 2.32 that encodes it admits a strictly positive solution \(t^{(S)}>0\), and infeasible otherwise. Feasibility is the linear surrogate for realizability at one level: by Lemma 2.33, an SPD \(A\) that makes every slot \((S\cup\{v\},j,k)\) of the family active, with both legs \((A/A_S)_{vj}\) and \((A/A_S)_{vk}\) nonzero, makes it feasible (take \(t^{(S)}\) to be the log-magnitudes of \(A/A_S\)), so infeasibility refutes realizability by such matrices. Without nonzero legs Lemma 2.33 supplies no equation.

2.7. The additive parametrization

Definition 2.32 (Partial correlation, log-magnitude, legs).

Let \(S\) be a level and \(j,k\in V\setminus S\) distinct with \((A/A_S)_{jk}\neq0\). The partial correlation is

\[r^{(S)}_{jk}=\frac{(A/A_S)_{jk}}{\sqrt{(A/A_S)_{jj}(A/A_S)_{kk}}},\]

and \(|r^{(S)}_{jk}|<1\) because the \(2\times2\) principal minor \((A/A_S)_{jj}(A/A_S)_{kk}-(A/A_S)_{jk}^2\) of the SPD matrix \(A/A_S\) is positive. Put \(t^{(S)}_{jk}=-\log|r^{(S)}_{jk}|\in(0,\infty)\) for such pairs, with the convention \(t^{(S)}_{jk}=+\infty\) when \((A/A_S)_{jk}=0\); in particular \(\log0\) is never taken, and \(t^{(S)}_{jk}>0\) is exactly strict definiteness on the pair \(\{j,k\}\).

Definition logMag 
variable {n : ℕ}

    (S : Finset (Fin n)) (j k : Fin n) : ℝ :=
  schurEntry A S j k
    / Real.sqrt (schurEntry A S j j * schurEntry A S k k)

noncomputable def logMag (A : Matrix (Fin n) (Fin n) ℝ)
    (S : Finset (Fin n)) (j k : Fin n) : ℝ :=
  - Real.log |partialCorr A S j k|

Every use guards this away by assuming the entries in play are nonzero, where the two conventions agree and \(t > 0\); no lemma relies on the totalised value \(0\).

Lemma 2.33 (Cancellation is additive in \(t\)).

Let \(S\) be a level, \(v\) a survivor, and \(j,k\) survivors distinct from \(v\) whose legs \((A/A_S)_{vj}\) and \((A/A_S)_{vk}\) are both nonzero (this is what “legs nonzero” means: the two entries of the pivot column at \(v\) used in the update of the pair \(\{j,k\}\), in the sense of Definition 2.6). Then

\[(A/A_{S\cup\{v\}})_{jk}=0 \iff r^{(S)}_{jk}=r^{(S)}_{vj}\,r^{(S)}_{vk},\]

and in that case

\[t^{(S)}_{jk}=t^{(S)}_{vj}+t^{(S)}_{vk}.\]

Conversely the additive relation together with the sign condition \(\operatorname{sgn}r^{(S)}_{jk}=\operatorname{sgn}r^{(S)}_{vj}\operatorname{sgn}r^{(S)}_{vk}\) implies the cancellation.

Proof.

Divide the update

\[(A/A_{S\cup\{v\}})_{jk}=(A/A_S)_{jk}-\frac{(A/A_S)_{vj}(A/A_S)_{vk}}{(A/A_S)_{vv}}\]

by \(\sqrt{(A/A_S)_{jj}(A/A_S)_{kk}}\), which is positive because \(A/A_S\) is SPD. The first term becomes \(r^{(S)}_{jk}\) by Definition 2.32. For the second, observe that each leg carries a factor \(\sqrt{(A/A_S)_{vv}}\) in its own normalisation, so that

\[r^{(S)}_{vj}r^{(S)}_{vk} =\frac{(A/A_S)_{vj}}{\sqrt{(A/A_S)_{vv}(A/A_S)_{jj}}}\cdot \frac{(A/A_S)_{vk}}{\sqrt{(A/A_S)_{vv}(A/A_S)_{kk}}} =\frac{(A/A_S)_{vj}(A/A_S)_{vk}}{(A/A_S)_{vv}\sqrt{(A/A_S)_{jj}(A/A_S)_{kk}}},\]

which is exactly the normalised correction term: the single power of \((A/A_S)_{vv}\) appearing in the update is absorbed by the two half-powers contributed by the legs, so no normalisation of the diagonal is needed. Hence the normalised update equals \(r^{(S)}_{jk}-r^{(S)}_{vj}r^{(S)}_{vk}\), and since the divisor is nonzero the first equivalence follows. Taking \(-\log|\cdot|\) of \(r^{(S)}_{jk}=r^{(S)}_{vj}r^{(S)}_{vk}\) and using Definition 2.32 gives the additive relation. For the converse, the additive relation determines the magnitudes only, so it yields the cancellation precisely when the signs agree; that is the stated condition on \(\operatorname{sgn}r\), a linear condition over \(\mathrm{GF}(2)\) independent of the magnitudes.

Remark 2.34 (How the additive system is used).

A family of slots at a level \(S\) imposes, by Lemma 2.33, a system of linear equations \(Tt^{(S)}=0\) in the unknowns \(t^{(S)}_{jk}>0\), one row per slot, on every realizing matrix whose legs are nonzero. 3. Core lemmas

3.1. Roadmap and Stiemke

Our infeasibility arguments all rest on a single linear-algebraic alternative. Let \(T\) be a real matrix. Stiemke's lemma [Sti15, Gor73] states that exactly one of the following holds: either there is a strictly positive vector \(t>0\) with \(Tt=0\), or there is a vector \(y\) such that \(y^\top T\ge0\) and \(y^\top T\neq0\). We use the alternative in the second direction. To show that an additive system \(Tt=0\) has no strictly positive solution, it suffices to exhibit one such \(y\), and because \(T\) has integer entries in all of our applications, \(y\) may be taken with integer entries as well. Each \(y\) is therefore an exact rational certificate that can be checked by hand, with no appeal to floating-point computation. The lemmas of this section fall into four families, which we describe in turn. The first family derives local obstructions from the positivity of \(2\times2\) principal minors. The Exchange Lemma 3.1 shows that two cancellation routes through the same pair of vertices cannot both occur. The Triangle Lemma 3.2 applies this to a triangle and concludes that at most one of its three apex routes can cancel, so at least two of its vertices cancel no pair of the triangle. The Max-Edge Lemma 3.3 turns the same inequality into a pivot selection rule: the pair of largest log-magnitude supplies a pivot whose partner survives. The second family consists of maximality arguments that produce safe pivots at a given level. Dense Safety 3.6 shows that a universal vertex cannot cancel the pair of largest log-magnitude incident to it, and the Universal Successor Lemma 3.8 shows that the weakest neighbour of a universal vertex is itself universal after that vertex is eliminated. Together they let a strategy keep choosing universal pivots. The third family governs how zeros persist, propagate, and are destroyed as the level grows. The Zero-leg Lemma 3.4 shows that when one leg of a route vanishes the corresponding entry is unchanged by the elimination, so a zero already present is carried forward. The Prefix Lemma 3.10 runs this backwards: a zero at a predicted-nonzero position must originate at an apex, which yields Corollary 3.11, that only an edge lying in a triangle of the fill graph can ever cancel. In the opposite direction, Fact 1 3.5 shows that eliminating a universal vertex destroys every pre-existing zero among the survivors, because the update contributes a nonzero product; this is the refill mechanism in its simplest form. The Refill Lemma 3.9 is its structural counterpart under the hypothesis \(\omega(G_A)\le3\): a cancelled pair lying in a second triangle forces the two apexes to be non-adjacent, since otherwise the four vertices would form a \(K_4\), and the second apex then restores the entry. Finally, Fresh fill 3.12 shows that a fill edge is nonzero at the moment it is created, though it may cancel at a later level, and the Nesting Lemma 3.14 shows that a pair cannot be zero at two consecutive levels without one of its legs vanishing. The fourth family establishes infeasibility of the additive system and the monotonicity that makes exhaustive search sound. A cancellation relation reads \(t_{jk}=t_{ji}+t_{ki}\) with all terms positive, so each relation strictly decreases the log-magnitude along the arcs of the digraph of Definition 3.15. The General Cycle Lemma 3.16 concludes that a directed cycle is impossible, since it would give \(t_x>\dots>t_x\). The 4-cycle Lemma 3.17 is the instance of this used in the pairing classification of Lemma 5.5. The Monotone Lemma 3.18 states that unrealizability is inherited by supersets of a slot family, which is what allows the search to restrict attention to minimal blocking families. Underlying all of these is the quotient property of Schur complements, Lemma 2.4, which is what licenses the staged computations: eliminating a level and then one further vertex agrees with eliminating the enlarged level in one step. In summary, the dependencies run Exchange \(\to\) Triangle \(\to\) Max-Edge \(\to\) Dense Safety and Universal Successor; Zero-leg \(\to\) Prefix \(\to\) Fresh fill and Nesting; General Cycle \(\to\) 4-cycle \(\to\) Pairing Lemma; and the Monotone Lemma closes the search.

Lemma 3.1 (Exchange Lemma).

Let \(A\succ0\), \(u,j,k\) distinct, \(A_{uk}\neq0\). If \(A_{uu}A_{jk}=A_{uj}A_{uk}\) then \(A_{jj}A_{uk}\neq A_{ju}A_{jk}\).

Lemma exchange 
variable {n : ℕ}
theorem exchange (A : Matrix (Fin n) (Fin n) ℝ) (hA : A.PosDef)
    (u j k : Fin n) (huj : u ≠ j) (_huk : u ≠ k) (_hjk : j ≠ k)
    (hak : A u k ≠ 0) (h1 : A u u * A j k = A u j * A u k) :
    A j j * A u k ≠ A j u * A j k

Lean source

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that

\[A_{jj}A_{uk} = A_{ju}A_{jk}. \qquad (2)\]

If \(A_{jk}\neq0\), then \(A_{jk}=A_{uj}A_{uk}/A_{uu}\). Insert this value into (2) and simplify, we get \(A_{uu}A_{jj}=A_{uj}^2\). This contradicts the corresponding \(2\times2\) minor has determinant \(>0\). If \(A_{jk}=0\), then since \(A_{uk}\neq 0\), \(A_{uj}=0\). But then (2) implies \(A_{jj}A_{uk}=0\). This is a contradiction because \(A_{uk}\neq 0\) by assumption and \(A_{jj}\neq 0\) because it is a diagonal entry of an SPD matrix.

Lemma 3.2 (Triangle Lemma).

Let \(\{j,k,u\}\) be a triangle of \(G_A\) whose three off-diagonal entries are all nonzero. Then at most one of its three apex routes cancels, and consequently at least two of \(j,k,u\) cancel no pair of the triangle.

Lemma triangle 
variable {n : ℕ}
theorem triangle (A : Matrix (Fin n) (Fin n) ℝ) (hA : A.PosDef)
    (j k u : Fin n) (hjk : j ≠ k) (hju : j ≠ u) (hku : k ≠ u)
    (_hjk' : A j k ≠ 0) (huk' : A u k ≠ 0) (huj' : A u j ≠ 0) :
    (¬ (Zeroes A ∅ u j k ∧ Zeroes A ∅ j u k)
        ∧ ¬ (Zeroes A ∅ u j k ∧ Zeroes A ∅ k u j)
        ∧ ¬ (Zeroes A ∅ j u k ∧ Zeroes A ∅ k u j))
      ∧ ∃ x ∈ ({j, k, u} : Finset (Fin n)),
        ∃ y ∈ ({j, k, u} : Finset (Fin n)), x ≠ y ∧
          IsCleanApexFor A ∅ x {j, k, u}
            ∧ IsCleanApexFor A ∅ y {j, k, u}

Lean source

Proof.

The pairs of the triangle are \(\{j,k\}\), \(\{u,k\}\) and \(\{u,j\}\), and a vertex can cancel only the pair opposite to it, since by Definition 2.19 the pair cancelled by a pivot consists of two other survivors. There are therefore exactly three apex routes to consider: \(u\) cancelling \((j,k)\), \(j\) cancelling \((u,k)\), and \(k\) cancelling \((u,j)\).

Suppose \(u\) cancels \((j,k)\), that is, \(A_{uu}A_{jk}=A_{uj}A_{uk}\). Applying Lemma 3.1 to the triple \((u,j,k)\), whose hypothesis \(A_{uk}\neq0\) holds by assumption, we obtain \(A_{jj}A_{uk}\neq A_{ju}A_{jk}\), which says precisely that \(j\) does not cancel \((u,k)\). Applying the same lemma to the triple \((u,k,j)\), whose hypothesis \(A_{uj}\neq0\) also holds, we obtain \(A_{kk}A_{uj}\neq A_{ku}A_{kj}\), so \(k\) does not cancel \((u,j)\). Hence if one apex route cancels the other two do not, which is the first assertion. For the second, the vertex whose route cancels is unique, so each of the remaining two cancels no pair of the triangle.

Lemma 3.3 (Max-Edge Lemma).

Let \(A\succ0\) have all off-diagonal entries nonzero, and let \((p,q)\) maximize \(t^{(\emptyset)}_{pq}\) over all nonzero pairs. Then no pair of \(C_p\) contains \(q\); consequently \(q\) survives the elimination of \(p\).

Lemma max_edge 
variable {n : ℕ}
theorem max_edge (A : Matrix (Fin n) (Fin n) ℝ) (hA : A.PosDef)
    (hdense : ∀ i j : Fin n, i ≠ j → A i j ≠ 0)
    (p q : Fin n) (hpq : p ≠ q)
    (hmax : ∀ i j : Fin n, i ≠ j → logMag A ∅ i j ≤ logMag A ∅ p q) :
    ¬ Covers A ∅ p q ∧ Survives A ∅ p q

Lean source

Proof.

Write \(t_{\max}=t^{(\emptyset)}_{pq}\) and suppose, for contradiction, that some pair of \(C_p\) contains \(q\), say \((q,k)\in C_p\) with \(k\notin\{p,q\}\). By Lemma 2.33 a cancellation is additive in the log-magnitudes, so

\[t^{(\emptyset)}_{qk}=t^{(\emptyset)}_{pq}+t^{(\emptyset)}_{pk}.\]

Every off-diagonal entry of \(A\) is nonzero, so \(t^{(\emptyset)}_{pk}\) is finite and strictly positive by Definition 2.32, whence \(t^{(\emptyset)}_{qk}>t_{\max}\). But \(\{q,k\}\) is itself a nonzero pair, so \(t^{(\emptyset)}_{qk}\le t_{\max}\) by the maximality of \((p,q)\). This contradiction shows that no pair of \(C_p\) contains \(q\).

For the consequence, let \(k\) be any survivor with \(\{q,k\}\) predicted nonzero after \(p\) is pivoted. By Definition 2.19 we have \((A/A_{\{p\}})_{qk}=0\) if and only if \((q,k)\in C_p\), and we have just excluded that. Hence every such entry remains nonzero, which is what it means for \(q\) to survive the elimination of \(p\).

□

Lemma 3.4 (Zero-leg equivalence).

Let \(S\) be a level and let \(j,k,v\in V\setminus S\) be distinct. If \((A/A_S)_{jv}=0\) then \((A/A_{S\cup\{v\}})_{jk}=(A/A_S)_{jk}\). Consequently, writing \(a=(S,j,v)\), \(b=(S,j,k)\) and \(c=(S\cup\{v\},j,k)\): if \(a\) is active, then \(b\) is active if and only if \(c\) is.

Lemma zero_leg 
variable {n : ℕ}
theorem zero_leg (A : Matrix (Fin n) (Fin n) ℝ) (hA : A.PosDef)
    (S : Finset (Fin n)) (j k v : Fin n)

    (hjv : j ≠ v) (hkv : k ≠ v) (hleg : schurEntry A S j v = 0) :
    schurEntry A (insert v S) j k = schurEntry A S j k ∧
      (schurEntry A S j k = 0 ↔ schurEntry A (insert v S) j k = 0)

Lean source

Proof.

By Definition 2.6, pivoting at \(v\) gives

\[(A/A_{S\cup\{v\}})_{jk}=(A/A_S)_{jk}-\frac{(A/A_S)_{jv}(A/A_S)_{kv}}{(A/A_S)_{vv}},\]

where the pivot value \((A/A_S)_{vv}\) is positive because \(A/A_S\) is positive definite. The hypothesis \((A/A_S)_{jv}=0\) makes the numerator of the correction term vanish, so the correction term is \(0\) and the two entries agree.

For the consequence, suppose \(a\) is active, that is \((A/A_S)_{jv}=0\). Then \(b\) and \(c\) assert that the same number is zero, namely \((A/A_S)_{jk}=(A/A_{S\cup\{v\}})_{jk}\), so one of them is active exactly when the other is.

Lemma 3.5 (Fact 1: a universal pivot destroys every pre-existing zero).

Let \(S\) be a level and let \(v\) be universal in \(M=A/A_S\) (Definition 2.21). If \(j,k\) are survivors distinct from \(v\) with \(M_{jk}=0\), then

\[(M/M_{vv})_{jk}=-\frac{M_{jv}M_{kv}}{M_{vv}}\neq0.\]

Consequently the set of zero pairs after pivoting at \(v\) is exactly \(C_v(S)\).

Hence no pre-existing zero survives the pivot, and the only zeros present afterwards are those the pivot itself creates, which by Definition 2.19 are exactly the pairs of \(C_v(S)\). Lemma 3.6 (Dense Safety).

Let \(M=A/A_S\) be such that every pair of survivors \(R=V\setminus S\) is nonzero in \(M\), and let \(\{p,q\}\) maximize \(t^{(S)}_{pq}\) over the pairs with \(M_{pq}\neq0\). Then \(C_p(S)\) does not cover \(q\).

      logMag A S j k ≤ logMag A S p q) :
    ¬ Covers A S p q

Proof.

Write \(t_{\max}=t^{(S)}_{pq}\) and suppose that \(C_p(S)\) covers \(q\), so that \((q,k)\in C_p(S)\) for some survivor \(k\notin\{p,q\}\). Lemma 3.8 (Universal Successor).

Let \(M=A/A_S\) with survivors \(R\), let \(p\) be universal in \(M\), and let \(q\in R\setminus\{p\}\) be such that \(p\) is a weakest neighbour of \(q\), that is \(t^{(S)}_{pq}=\max\{t^{(S)}_{qx}:M_{qx}\neq0\}\). Then \(q\) is universal in \(M/M_{pp}\).

Lemma 3.9 (Refill Lemma).

Assume \(\omega(G_A)\le3\). Let \(\{j,k,u\}\) and \(\{j,k,w\}\) be two distinct triangles of \(G_A\) sharing the edge \(\{j,k\}\), so that \(A_{jw}\neq0\) and \(A_{kw}\neq0\), and suppose that \(u\) cancels \((j,k)\). Then \(A_{uw}=0\); moreover, writing \(M=A/A_{\{u\}}\), we have \(M_{jw}=A_{jw}\neq0\) and \(M_{kw}=A_{kw}\neq0\), and

\[(M/M_{ww})_{jk}=-\frac{A_{jw}A_{kw}}{M_{ww}}\neq0.\]

That is, pivoting at \(w\) restores the entry that \(u\) destroyed.

Lemma refill

    (hwj : w ≠ j) (hwk : w ≠ k) (hwu : w ≠ u)
    (hjkA : (patternGraph A).Adj j k) (hjuA : (patternGraph A).Adj j u)
    (hkuA : (patternGraph A).Adj k u) (hjwA : (patternGraph A).Adj j w)
    (hkwA : (patternGraph A).Adj k w) (hz : Zeroes A ∅ u j k) :
    A u w = 0 ∧ schurEntry A {u} j w = A j w ∧
      schurEntry A {u} j w ≠ 0 ∧ schurEntry A {u} k w = A k w ∧
      schurEntry A {u} k w ≠ 0 ∧
      schurEntry A (insert w {u}) j k
        = - (A j w * A k w / schurEntry A {u} w w) ∧
      schurEntry A (insert w {u}) j k ≠ 0

Lean source

Proof.

Suppose \(A_{uw}\neq0\). The six pairs among \(u,j,k,w\) would then all be nonzero: \(\{j,k\}\), \(\{u,j\}\) and \(\{u,k\}\) because \(\{j,k,u\}\) is a triangle, \(\{j,w\}\) and \(\{k,w\}\) because \(\{j,k,w\}\) is a triangle, and \(\{u,w\}\) by supposition. Thus \(\{u,j,k,w\}\) would be a clique of \(G_A\) on four vertices, giving \(\omega(G_A)\ge4\) and contradicting the hypothesis. Hence \(A_{uw}=0\).

Now pivot at \(u\). By Definition 2.6, \(M_{jw}=A_{jw}-A_{ju}A_{wu}/A_{uu}=A_{jw}\), since \(A_{wu}=A_{uw}=0\), and likewise \(M_{kw}=A_{kw}\); both are nonzero by hypothesis. Since \(u\) cancels \((j,k)\) we have \(M_{jk}=0\). Pivoting at \(w\) therefore gives

\[(M/M_{ww})_{jk}=M_{jk}-\frac{M_{jw}M_{kw}}{M_{ww}}=-\frac{A_{jw}A_{kw}}{M_{ww}},\]

which is nonzero because the two legs are nonzero and \(M_{ww}>0\) by positive definiteness of \(M\).

Lemma 3.10 (Prefix Lemma).

Let \(S\) be a level and suppose \((A/A_S)_{jk}=0\) for a pair with \(\{j,k\}\in G_S\). Then there exists \(v\in S\) with \(\{j,v\},\{k,v\}\in G_{S\setminus\{v\}}\).

Lemma prefix_lemma 
variable {n : ℕ}
theorem prefix_lemma (A : Matrix (Fin n) (Fin n) ℝ) (hA : A.PosDef)
    (l : List (Fin n)) (S : Finset (Fin n)) (henum : Enumerates l S)
    (j k : Fin n) (hj : j ∉ S) (hk : k ∉ S)
    (hz : schurEntry A S j k = 0)
    (hg : (gameGraph (patternGraph A) l).Adj j k) :
    ∃ v ∈ S, ∃ l' : List (Fin n), Enumerates l' (S.erase v) ∧
      (gameGraph (patternGraph A) l').Adj j v ∧
      (gameGraph (patternGraph A) l').Adj k v

Lean source

Proof.

We argue by induction on \(|S|\).

If \(S=\emptyset\) the hypothesis is vacuous: \(\{j,k\}\in G_\emptyset=G_A\) means \(A_{jk}\neq0\), whereas \((A/A_\emptyset)_{jk}=A_{jk}=0\), so there is nothing to prove.

Let \(|S|\ge1\), write \(S=S'\cup\{v\}\) where \(v\) is the vertex of \(S\) eliminated last, and recall from Lemma 2.4 that this decomposition may be made for any choice of last vertex. There are two cases.

If \(\{j,v\}\in G_{S'}\) and \(\{k,v\}\in G_{S'}\), then \(v\) is the required witness, because \(S\setminus\{v\}=S'\).

Otherwise one of the two pairs is absent from \(G_{S'}\); by the symmetry of \(j\) and \(k\) we may assume \(\{j,v\}\notin G_{S'}\). Since the numeric pattern is contained in the symbolic one (Definition 2.12), this forces \((A/A_{S'})_{jv}=0\), so Lemma 3.4 gives \((A/A_S)_{jk}=(A/A_{S'})_{jk}\), and the zero was therefore already present at level \(S'\). We claim moreover that \(\{j,k\}\in G_{S'}\). Indeed, if \(\{j,k\}\notin G_{S'}\) then \(\{j,k\}\) is a fill edge created by \(v\), which by the definition of the elimination game requires both \(\{j,v\}\) and \(\{k,v\}\) to lie in \(G_{S'}\), contrary to the case assumption. The inductive hypothesis applied at level \(S'\) now yields a vertex \(v'\in S'\) with \(\{j,v'\},\{k,v'\}\in G_{S'\setminus\{v'\}}\), and \(v'\in S\), which completes the induction.

Lemma 3.12 (Fresh fill is nonzero when created).

Let \(S\) be a level, let \(v\in V\setminus S\), and let \(j,k\in V\setminus(S\cup\{v\})\) be distinct with \(\{j,v\},\{k,v\}\in G_S\) but \(\{j,k\}\notin G_S\), so that \(\{j,k\}\) is a fill edge created by \(v\). Then \((A/A_S)_{jk}=0\), and

\[(A/A_{S\cup\{v\}})_{jk}=-\frac{(A/A_S)_{jv}(A/A_S)_{kv}}{(A/A_S)_{vv}} .\]

If in addition both legs are nonzero, that is \((A/A_S)_{jv}\neq0\) and \((A/A_S)_{kv}\neq0\), then this entry is nonzero: fresh fill is nonzero at the moment of its creation. It may of course cancel at a later level.

Lemma 3.14 (Nesting Lemma).

Let \(S\) be a level and let \(i,j,k\in V\setminus S\) be distinct. If the slots \((S,j,k)\) and \((S\cup\{i\},j,k)\) are both active, then at least one of the slots \((S,j,i)\) and \((S,k,i)\) is active.

By Definition 2.6,

\[(A/A_{S\cup\{i\}})_{jk}=(A/A_S)_{jk}-\frac{(A/A_S)_{ji}(A/A_S)_{ki}}{(A/A_S)_{ii}} .\]

The left-hand side is zero because \((S\cup\{i\},j,k)\) is active, and the first term on the right is zero because \((S,j,k)\) is active. Hence \((A/A_S)_{ji}(A/A_S)_{ki}/(A/A_S)_{ii}=0\), and since the pivot value \((A/A_S)_{ii}\) is positive the product \((A/A_S)_{ji}(A/A_S)_{ki}\) vanishes. A product of real numbers vanishes only if one factor does, so \((A/A_S)_{ji}=0\) or \((A/A_S)_{ki}=0\), which says that \((S,j,i)\) or \((S,k,i)\) is active.

Proof.

For \(j,k\notin S\), the Schur complement has the standard almost-principal-minor form

\[(A/A_S)_{jk}= \frac{\det A_{S\cup\{j\},\,S\cup\{k\}}}{\det A_{S,S}},\]

where \(A_{I,J}\) denotes the submatrix with rows \(I\) and columns \(J\); \(A_{S,S}\) is principal. In particular the expression is manifestly independent of the elimination order on \(S\).

Assume \(G_A\) is a forest. Then any two vertices of \(G_A\) are joined by at most one simple path: none if they lie in different components, and exactly one otherwise. By Proposition 2.10 the hypothesis \(\{j,k\}\in G_S\) supplies such a path, so in the situation of interest the \(j\)–\(k\) path exists and is unique; we write \(\mathrm{path}(j,k)\) for its edge set and \(I\) for its interior, and \(I\subseteq S\). Fix \(j,k\notin S\) with \(\{j,k\}\in G_S\) (symbolic). Let \(I\) be that interior, \(I\subseteq S\), and let \(\mathrm{path}(j,k)\) be the edge set of the unique \(j\)–\(k\) path. A term of \(\det A_{S\cup\{j\},S\cup\{k\}}\) is \(\pm\prod_{u}A_{u,\pi(u)}\) for a bijection \(\pi:S\cup\{j\}\to S\cup\{k\}\), and it is nonzero only if \(\{u,\pi(u)\}\in E(G_A)\) or \(u=\pi(u)\) for every \(u\). Regard \(\pi\) as a partial permutation of \(V\): it is defined on \(S\cup\{j\}\) and takes values in \(S\cup\{k\}\), so the two indices that fail to match are \(j\) (a source, not in the codomain) and \(k\) (a sink, not in the domain). Decomposing \(\pi\) accordingly, it consists of one directed path from \(j\) to \(k\) through vertices of \(S\), together with fixed points and cycles on the remaining indices of \(S\). The path contributes \(\prod_{e}A_e\) over the edges of a \(j\)–\(k\) walk in \(G_A\) with interior in \(S\); in a forest the only such walk without repeated vertices is \(\mathrm{path}(j,k)\). The fixed points and cycles involve only indices of \(S\setminus I\) and are matched among themselves, and their total contribution is precisely \(\det A_{S\setminus I,S\setminus I}\). Note that this includes \(2\)-cycles \(A_{uv}A_{vu}=A_{uv}^2\) along genuine forest edges: such terms are legitimate and are exactly what the determinant \(\det A_{S\setminus I,S\setminus I}\) collects, which is why no claim is made that off-path indices cannot be moved. After fixing that \(j\)–\(k\) route, the remaining indices \(S\setminus I\) must permute among themselves, contributing a factor \(\det A_{S\setminus I,S\setminus I}\), while off-path \(2\)-cycles \(A_{uv}^2\) using genuine forest edges are collected exactly in that principal minor. Hence

\[\det A_{S\cup\{j\},S\cup\{k\}} = \pm\Bigl(\prod_{e\in\mathrm{path}(j,k)} A_e\Bigr)\,\det A_{S\setminus I,S\setminus I},\]

and therefore

\[(A/A_S)_{jk}= \pm\frac{\bigl(\prod_{e\in\mathrm{path}(j,k)} A_e\bigr)\,\det A_{S\setminus I}}{\det A_{S}}.\]

This is the Jones–West path formula [JW05], see Lemma 1 there for the precise expansion; both \(\det A_{S\setminus I}\) and \(\det A_{S}\) are principal minors of the SPD matrix \(A\), hence \(>0\), and every \(A_e\neq0\) by the forest pattern. Thus \((A/A_S)_{jk}\neq0\). Vertices of \(S\) off the path are accounted for automatically via \(\det A_{S\setminus I}\), which collects all their internal matchings, and they only affect pivots, never the numerator product \(\prod_{e\in\mathrm{path}(j,k)} A_e\), which is manifestly order-independent; Figure 2 illustrates the expansion on a small forest. No accidental zero ever occurs, irrespective of ordering [MR11, JW05].

Definition 4.3 (2-path strip).

For \(k\ge3\), the \(2\)-path strip on vertices \(v_1,\dots,v_k\) is the graph \(P\) whose edge set is exactly

the first family called boundary edges and the second diagonal edges; its triangles are \(T_i=\{v_i,v_{i+1},v_{i+2}\}\) for \(1\le i\le k-2\). We say \(G_A\) is a \(2\)-path strip if \(G_A=P\) for some such labelling; in particular all listed edges are original pattern edges, not fill.

Definition 4.4 (Strip state).

A strip state is a pair \((S,P)\) where \(S\subseteq V\) is a level (Remark 2.5) and \(P\) is the graph that \(G_S\) induces on the survivors \(V\setminus S\), assumed to be a \(2\)-path strip on \(k=|V\setminus S|\) vertices. Note that \(P\) is taken in the symbolic graph \(G_S\), not in \(G_A\): after a SECOND move the strip uses a fill edge. We write its left end as \(s_1=v_1,s_2=v_2,s_3=v_3,s_4=v_4\); this names four particular survivors and carries no assumption that \(k=4\) (the right end is symmetric, and we treat only the left). For a \(2\)-path strip this \(P\) is itself a \(2\)-path strip, except after a SECOND move it uses the fill edge \((s_1,s_4)\), which is not an edge of \(G_A\) but is an edge of \(G_S\) by Lemma 3.12. The state is

  • State 0 if every boundary and diagonal edge of \(P\) (including the fill edge \((s_1,s_4)\) when present) is nonzero in \(A/A_S\);
  • State 1 if exactly one pair is zero in \(A/A_S\), namely \((s_2,s_3)\), and every other boundary and diagonal edge of \(P\) is nonzero in \(A/A_S\).

Proof of (i), strips and fans.

We prove by induction on \(k=|V\setminus S|\) that a strip state \((S,P)\) admits a clean continuation and that States 0 and 1 are preserved. For \(k=3\) there are two cases, according to the state. In State 0 all three pairs of the triangle \(\{s_1,s_2,s_3\}\) are nonzero, so Lemma 3.2 applies: at most one apex route cancels, and at least two of the three vertices zero no pair of the triangle. Pivot such a clean apex; the pivot is clean because its two entries among the survivors are nonzero, and the surviving pair is nonzero (otherwise it would lie in the cancellation graph of that apex), so the final \(2\times2\) Schur complement is clean. In State 1 the pair \((s_2,s_3)\) is zero while \((s_1,s_2)\) and \((s_1,s_3)\) are nonzero, so Lemma 3.2 does not apply and the argument just given is unavailable. Pivot \(s_1\) instead: it is clean because both of its entries among the survivors are nonzero, and it restores \((s_2,s_3)\) by the computation displayed in the State 1 case below, leaving a \(2\times2\) Schur complement whose off-diagonal entry is nonzero. Both cases are needed, because a State 1 strip on three vertices does arise: a SECOND move at \(k=4\) can leave the exceptional zero at the new \((s_2,s_3)\).

Inductive step: let \((S,P)\) be a strip state with left end \(s_1=v_1,s_2=v_2,s_3=v_3,s_4=v_4\) (right end symmetric), \(k\ge4\). Distinguish:

  • State 0 and \((s_2,s_3)\notin C_{s_1}(S)\): END at \(s_1\). Since \(N(s_1)=\{s_2,s_3\}\) is an edge, no fill is created and the only pair updated is \((s_2,s_3)\), which is nonzero by hypothesis \((s_2,s_3)\notin C_{s_1}(S)\). The pivot reads \((s_1,s_2),(s_1,s_3)\), both nonzero in State 0, hence clean. Result is State 0 on \(k-1\) vertices.
  • State 0 and \((s_2,s_3)\in C_{s_1}(S)\): SECOND at \(s_2\). Note \((s_2,s_3)\) is nonzero at the time \(s_2\) is pivoted, because \(s_1\) has not been eliminated yet — this subtle point ensures the pivot itself is clean. The pairs updated are \((s_1,s_3)\), \((s_1,s_4)\) (fill), \((s_3,s_4)\). Lemma 3.12 gives \((s_1,s_4)=-(A/A_S)_{s_2,s_1}(A/A_S)_{s_2,s_4}/(A/A_S)_{s_2s_2}\neq0\). Since \(s_1\) zeroes \((s_2,s_3)\) by assumption, Lemma 3.2 in triangle \(\{s_1,s_2,s_3\}\) implies \(s_2\) cannot zero \((s_1,s_3)\); hence \((s_1,s_3)\neq0\) after \(s_2\). Only \((s_3,s_4)\) may become zero, which is exactly the new \((s_2,s_3)\) in the remaining strip with vertices \(s_1,s_3,s_4,s_5,\dots\). Thus we move to State 0 if \((s_3,s_4)\) stays nonzero, otherwise to State 1 with zero at new \((s_2,s_3)\). In either case the pivot \(s_2\) is clean because \((s_2,s_1),(s_2,s_3),(s_2,s_4)\) are all nonzero in State 0 before \(s_1\) is gone.
  • State 1: END at \(s_1\). The exceptional zero is at \((s_2,s_3)\), not at \((s_1,s_2)\). Removing \(s_1\) does not remove that pair; if we pivoted \(s_2\) next we would commit a zero to \(L\) and be dirty. Instead END refills it: \[ (A/A_{S\cup\{s_1\}})_{s_2s_3}=0-\frac{(A/A_S)_{s_1s_2}(A/A_S)_{s_1s_3}}{(A/A_S)_{s_1s_1}}\neq0, \] because both legs are nonzero in State 1 by definition and \((A/A_S)_{s_1s_1}>0\) by positive definiteness. This is the pivot update of Definition 2.6 applied to a pair that is already zero, the same computation that appears in Lemma 3.5 and in Lemma 3.12. The pivot \(s_1\) reads \((s_1,s_2),(s_1,s_3)\), both nonzero, hence clean. After END the state is State 0 on \(k-1\) vertices.

Proof of (ii), one-triangle.

First we prove the positive claim for all chordal graphs with exactly one triangle. Let \(G\) be chordal with exactly one triangle \(T\). Every simplicial vertex \(v\notin T\) has exactly one neighbour: if \(v\) had two neighbours \(x\) and \(y\), they would be adjacent to each other because \(N(v)\) is a clique, so \(\{v,x,y\}\) would be a triangle, and it would be distinct from \(T\) because \(v\notin T\), contradicting the hypothesis that \(T\) is the only triangle. Hence eliminating \(v\) creates no fill and updates no off-diagonal pair: \((A/A_S)_{jk}\) is unchanged for \(j,k\neq v\), only the diagonal \((A/A_S)_{vv}\) disappears. Hence such a \(v\) cannot create a zero and can be peeled cleanly irrespective of numerics. Moreover, as long as \(G\) properly contains \(T\) there is a simplicial vertex outside \(T\) available to peel: a chordal graph with exactly one triangle consists of \(T\) with trees attached, since a cycle other than \(T\) would have a chord and so create a second triangle, and a leaf of such a tree is simplicial and outside \(T\). Peeling these vertices one at a time therefore leaves exactly \(T\), a triangle, where Lemma 3.2 supplies a clean apex (at most one of the three vertices is dirty, so at least two are clean). Thus an adaptive clean ordering exists for every SPD matrix with that pattern.

Proof of (iii), edge-disjoint triangles.

Let \(G\) be chordal with \(\omega\le3\) and pairwise edge-disjoint triangles. Use the bipartite block-cut tree (blocks vs cut vertices) whose leaves are always blocks.

Consider a leaf block \(B\) in this tree. If \(B\) is an edge \(\{c,x\}\) where \(c\) is the cut vertex, then \(x\) is simplicial with \(N(x)=\{c\}\); eliminating \(x\) creates no fill and updates no pair, so it is clean and leaves a graph in the same class.

If \(B\) is a triangle \(\{c,a,b\}\) where \(c\) is the cut vertex (or \(B\) is isolated triangle when \(G\) itself is a triangle), then \(a,b\) are simplicial and \(N(a)=\{c,b\}\), \(N(b)=\{c,a\}\) are edges. By Lemma 3.2 at most one of the three vertices of \(B\) is dirty (zeroes a pair of \(B\)), so at least one of the two simplicial vertices \(a,b\) is clean — pigeonhole. Choose a clean apex among \(a,b\), say \(a\). Then \(a\) zeroes no pair of its triangle, its neighbourhood is exactly the opposite edge \(\{c,b\}\) because \(\omega\le3\) and edge-disjointness, so eliminating \(a\) creates no fill and zeroes no pair of \(B\). The remaining graph is still chordal with \(\omega\le3\) and edge-disjoint triangles, with \(|V|\) reduced by one (the triangle block \(\{c,a,b\}\) becomes the edge block \(\{c,b\}\), so the number of blocks is unchanged). Induction on \(|V|\) (equivalently \(|E|\)) closes.

Lemma 5.5 (Pairing Lemma).

Fix a level \(S\) and a set \(W\) of four surviving vertices. There are \(4\binom{3}{2}=12\) candidate slots \((u,\{j,k\})\) with \(u\in W\) and \(\{j,k\}\subseteq W\setminus\{u\}\), one for each choice of apex together with the pair it cancels, and \(\binom{4}{2}=6\) variables \(t^{(S)}_{jk}\), one for each pair inside \(W\). Then:

  1. every feasible set of these slots has at most four elements;
  2. a feasible set of four slots that uses all four vertices of \(W\) as apexes is a fixed-point-free involution of \(W\): the four vertices split into two pairs, and each vertex cancels the pair opposite its own;
  3. all three such configurations, one for each of the three partitions of \(W\) into two pairs, are feasible.

Proof.

Throughout, a slot \((u,p)\) with \(p=\{j,k\}\) imposes the relation \(t^{(S)}_{jk}=t^{(S)}_{uj}+t^{(S)}_{uk}\) of Lemma 2.33, and feasibility of a set of slots means that the resulting relations admit a solution with all six variables strictly positive.

(1) Size at most four. Map a slot \((u,p)\) to the ordered pair \((u,d)\), where \(d=W\setminus(\{u\}\cup p)\) is the unique vertex of \(W\) lying neither in \(p\) nor at the apex. Suppose two slots of a feasible set, say \((u,p)\) and \((v,q)\), have the same second coordinate \(d\). Since \(u\neq v\) and neither \(u\) nor \(v\) equals \(d\), the pair \(p=W\setminus\{u,d\}\) contains \(v\) and the pair \(q=W\setminus\{v,d\}\) contains \(u\). So \(u\) cancels a pair containing \(v\) while \(v\) cancels a pair containing \(u\), and Lemma 3.1 forbids exactly this. Hence the second coordinates of a feasible set are distinct, the set injects into \(W\), and its size is at most \(|W|=4\).

(2) Four slots using all four apexes. Let \(H\) be such a set. By the injectivity just proved its second coordinates are four distinct vertices, so they exhaust \(W\); by hypothesis its first coordinates exhaust \(W\) as well. Writing \(\pi(u)\) for the second coordinate of the slot with apex \(u\), we therefore obtain a permutation \(\pi\) of \(W\), and \(\pi\) has no fixed point because \(d\neq u\) by construction. A fixed-point-free permutation of a four-element set is either a product of two disjoint transpositions, of which there are three, or a four-cycle, of which there are six.

Suppose \(\pi\) is a four-cycle. Relabelling \(W\) as \(\{0,1,2,3\}\) we may assume \(\pi=(0\,1\,2\,3)\), so that each vertex \(u\) cancels the pair \(W\setminus\{u,\pi(u)\}\), and the four imposed relations are

\[t^{(S)}_{23}=t^{(S)}_{02}+t^{(S)}_{03},\qquad t^{(S)}_{03}=t^{(S)}_{01}+t^{(S)}_{13},\qquad t^{(S)}_{01}=t^{(S)}_{02}+t^{(S)}_{12},\qquad t^{(S)}_{12}=t^{(S)}_{13}+t^{(S)}_{23}.\]

Every variable is strictly positive, so each relation makes its left side strictly larger than either summand on its right. Reading the four relations in the order above gives

\[t^{(S)}_{12}>t^{(S)}_{23}>t^{(S)}_{03}>t^{(S)}_{01}>t^{(S)}_{12},\]

which is impossible. This is the four-cycle instance of the General Cycle Lemma 3.16, recorded as Lemma 3.17. Hence \(\pi\) is a product of two disjoint transpositions, which is precisely the assertion that \(W\) splits into two pairs with each vertex cancelling the opposite pair.

(3) The three involutions are feasible. By symmetry it suffices to treat the pairing \(\{0,1\},\{2,3\}\), whose four slots impose

\[t^{(S)}_{23}=t^{(S)}_{02}+t^{(S)}_{03},\qquad t^{(S)}_{23}=t^{(S)}_{12}+t^{(S)}_{13},\qquad t^{(S)}_{01}=t^{(S)}_{02}+t^{(S)}_{12},\qquad t^{(S)}_{01}=t^{(S)}_{03}+t^{(S)}_{13}.\]

Given any \(\alpha,\beta>0\), set \(t^{(S)}_{02}=t^{(S)}_{13}=\alpha\), \(t^{(S)}_{03}=t^{(S)}_{12}=\beta\) and \(t^{(S)}_{01}=t^{(S)}_{23}=\alpha+\beta\). All six values are positive and all four relations hold, so the configuration is feasible. (Corollary 5.6 shows that these are the only solutions.)

Finally, the hypothesis in (2) that all four vertices occur as apexes cannot be dropped: a feasible set of four slots may repeat an apex. For instance the four slots \((0,\{1,2\})\), \((0,\{1,3\})\), \((2,\{0,3\})\), \((2,\{1,3\})\) use only \(0\) and \(2\) as apexes, and they are feasible, since for arbitrary \(t^{(S)}_{01},t^{(S)}_{02},t^{(S)}_{23}>0\) the assignments \(t^{(S)}_{03}=t^{(S)}_{02}+t^{(S)}_{23}\), \(t^{(S)}_{12}=t^{(S)}_{01}+t^{(S)}_{02}\) and \(t^{(S)}_{13}=t^{(S)}_{01}+t^{(S)}_{02}+t^{(S)}_{23}\) satisfy all four relations. Such a configuration is of no use in blocking: a level at which some vertex never serves as a pivot leaves that vertex available, so it cannot spoil every ordering. This is why Theorem 5.8 needs the configurations of (2) rather than merely maximal feasible ones.

Remark 5.7 (Why the order must be even, and why odd orders resist).

A fixed-point-free involution exists only on a set of even cardinality, so the pairing of Lemma 5.5 is available at a level with four survivors precisely because \(4\) is even. Propagating the pairing structure to the whole vertex set therefore requires \(n\) even, which is what \(M_{2m}\) realizes; this is the source of the parity restriction in Theorem 5.8.

Odd orders \(n\ge7\) remain open, and it is worth recording exactly how little of the method survives there. The classification of Lemma 5.5 needs \(|W|=4\): its injectivity step fails at \(|W|=5\), where feasible sets using all five pivots do exist, a \(4\)-pairing together with any slot for the fifth pivot being feasible in all six cases. Parity therefore does not propagate, and odd orders enjoy no structural protection of the kind that forces the construction to be even. Two computations delimit the position further. At \(n=5\) the maximum feasible size is \(8\), whereas a scheme leaving one survivor at every vertex would need \(\binom{5}{2}=10\) relations, so no such scheme exists at order five; among the \(3^{10}=59049\) three-magnitude colorings the minimum total number of survivors is \(8\), and none leaves exactly one survivor at every vertex. At \(n=7\) a pruned depth-first search of \(280\) million nodes together with canonical augmentation (\(92\,423\) orbits at level 7, growth factor \(\approx4.7\)) failed to exhaust the space, so brute force is closed as a route; that search is not included in the ancillary material. We note in passing that a counting heuristic distinguishes the parities — a scheme leaving one survivor at every vertex imposes \(n\lceil(n-2)/2\rceil\) relations on \(\binom{n}{2}\) unknowns, which is underdetermined for even \(n\) and exactly square for odd \(n\), so that a generic square system would force every \(t^{(\emptyset)}_{jk}=0\) and hence \(|r^{(\emptyset)}|=1\), contradicting strict definiteness — but we attach no weight to it: the hypothesis of genericity fails precisely on the singular locus where \(M_{2m}\) and every other counterexample of this paper live.

Remark 5.9 (The parameter curve is a one-parameter family).

The hypothesis (4) is neither vacuous nor more restrictive than the curve itself. Fix \(B\in(0,1)\) and put \(f(A)=A^2+(1-B^2)A-1\). Then \(f(0)=-1<0\) and \(f\) is strictly increasing on \(A>0\), while \(f(1)=1-B^2>0\); hence \(f\) has exactly one root in \((0,1)\), namely

\[\alpha(B)=\frac{-(1-B^2)+\sqrt{(1-B^2)^2+4}}{2}\in(0,1).\]

Here \(\alpha(B)\) denotes the root of \(f\) regarded as a function of \(B\), not a product. Moreover \(\alpha(B)>B\) automatically. Indeed \(f\) is strictly increasing on \(A>0\) and vanishes at \(\alpha(B)\), so for \(x>0\) we have \(x<\alpha(B)\) if and only if \(f(x)<0\); taking \(x=B\) gives

\[f(B)=B^2+(1-B^2)B-1=-(B-1)^2(B+1)<0\qquad\text{for all }B\in(0,1),\]

whence \(B<\alpha(B)\). So the admissible parameters are exactly the pairs \((\alpha(B),B)\) with \(0<B<1\), and the inequalities \(0<B<A<1\) of (4) are consequences of the equation rather than extra assumptions. The endpoints are excluded for good reason: \(B=0\) would annihilate the opposite-parity entries, so that \(G_{M_{2m}}\) would no longer be complete, while \(B\to1\) forces \(A\to1\) and \(\lambda_3=(1-A)(1-B)\to0\).

Consequently Theorem 5.8 produces, at each even order \(n=2m\ge6\), a one-parameter family of counterexamples lying on a measure-zero algebraic curve, not a single matrix. The instance \(B=\tfrac12\), for which \(A=(-3+\sqrt{73})/8\approx0.693\) and \(AB\approx0.3465\), is the one used for the explicit verification described in Appendices A and F; a concrete instance is needed there only because exact arithmetic must be carried out in a specific field, here \(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{73})\). Whether the curve accounts for every counterexample at even order is a separate question, and an open one; we state it as Question 7.1.

Proof.

Throughout we use the two facts recorded in (4): that \(A\) lies strictly between \(B\) and \(1\), and that \(A\) is the root of \(A^2+(1-B^2)A-1=0\) in \((0,1)\). The first gives \(\lambda_2>0\) for every \(m\), since \(A-B>0\), and the remaining three eigenvalues are visibly positive; the second will be used only once, in step 5, where it is forced upon us rather than assumed. The diagonal of \(M_{2m}\) is the identity, so every pivot value at stage 1 equals \(1\) and the Schur update of Definition 2.6 reads \(S^{(1)}_{jk}=M_{jk}-M_{p_0j}M_{p_0k}\) for a first pivot \(p_0\).

Fix any first pivot. Since relabelling pairs is a symmetry of \(M_{2m}\), and since the two vertices of a pair play symmetric roles, we may assume the first pivot is \(p_0=2k_0\) for some \(k_0\); write \(q_0=2k_0+1\) for its partner. Let \(m\ge3\). Figure 5 shows the pair structure and the two stages of the argument for \(m=3\).

Step 1: the first pivot zeroes a perfect matching on the other pairs, for every choice of parameters. Let \(k\neq k_0\) and consider the within-pair position \((2k,2k+1)\). Here \(M_{2k,2k+1}=AB\), while \(M_{p_0,2k}=A\) because \(p_0\) and \(2k\) have equal parity, and \(M_{p_0,2k+1}=B\) because \(p_0\) and \(2k+1\) have opposite parity. Hence

\[S^{(1)}_{2k,2k+1}=AB-A\cdot B=0 .\]

This cancellation is an identity in \(A\) and \(B\): it holds for every choice of the two parameters, and no relation between them is required. Write

\[Z_{p_0}=\bigl\{\{2k,2k+1\}: k\neq k_0\bigr\}\]

for the resulting set of zero pairs, a perfect matching on the \(m-1\) pairs other than \(P_{k_0}\).

Step 2: the partner \(q_0\) meets none of these zeros. For \(k\neq k_0\) we have \(M_{q_0,2k}=B\) and \(M_{q_0,2k+1}=A\), while \(M_{q_0p_0}=AB\), so

\[S^{(1)}_{q_0,2k}=B-AB\cdot A=B(1-A^2)\neq0, \qquad S^{(1)}_{q_0,2k+1}=A-AB\cdot B=A(1-B^2)\neq0,\]

both nonzero because \(0<A<1\) and \(0<B<1\). Hence no zero created by the first pivot is incident to \(q_0\): writing \(Z_{p_0}\) for the graph on \(V/ \{p_0\}\) whose edges are the cancelled pairs, \(q_0\) is an isolated vertex of \(Z_{p_0}\), while every other survivor meets exactly one edge of it.

Step 3: no cross-pair position cancels. Let \(k\neq\ell\) with \(k,\ell\neq k_0\). There are three cases, according to parities.

  • Both even, \(p=2k\) and \(q=2\ell\): then \(M_{pq}=A\) and \(M_{p_0p}=M_{p_0q}=A\), so \(S^{(1)}_{pq}=A-A^2=A(1-A)\neq0\).
  • Mixed, \(p=2k\) and \(q=2\ell+1\): then \(M_{pq}=B\), \(M_{p_0p}=A\) and \(M_{p_0q}=B\), so \(S^{(1)}_{pq}=B-AB=B(1-A)\neq0\).
  • Both odd, \(p=2k+1\) and \(q=2\ell+1\): then \(M_{pq}=A\) and \(M_{p_0p}=M_{p_0q}=B\), so \(S^{(1)}_{pq}=A-B^2\). Rearranging (4) as \((1-B^2)A=1-A^2\) and dividing by \(A>0\) gives \[ A-B^2=\frac{1-A}{A}>0 , \] so this entry too is nonzero.

Together with step 2 this shows that \(Z_{p_0}\) is exactly the set of zeros of \(S^{(1)}\).

Step 4: the second pivot is forced to be \(q_0\). Suppose the second pivot is some \(p_1\neq q_0\), say \(p_1\in P_k\) with \(k\neq k_0\), and let \(p_1'\) be its partner, so that \(\{p_1,p_1'\}\in Z_{p_0}\) by step 1. Because \(G_A=K_{2m}\) is complete, this pair is predicted nonzero, and it is committed to \(L\) at the moment \(p_1\) is pivoted, that is at level \(S=\{p_0\}\), where its value is \(0\). Hence any such ordering already fails. Only \(p_1=q_0\) avoids an immediate failure, and by step 2 it does avoid one, since \(q_0\) meets no zero of \(S^{(1)}\).

It is worth being explicit about why the zeros of step 1 are not themselves failures of Conjecture 2.27. Under the factor definition of cleanliness a pair \(\{j,k\}\) is committed only at the level preceding the earlier of \(j\) and \(k\), so a zero in \(S^{(1)}\) costs nothing unless the second pivot lies in that pair; an intervening elimination may restore the entry, which is the refill phenomenon of Section 2. This is exactly why \(M_4\) has eight clean orderings while \(M_{2m}\) has none for \(m\ge3\).

Step 5: after the forced second pivot, every remaining vertex meets a zero — and this is where the parameter equation earns its place. Let \(k,\ell\neq k_0\) with \(k\neq\ell\). By steps 2 and 3,

\[S^{(1)}_{2k,2\ell+1}=B(1-A),\quad S^{(1)}_{q_0,2k}=B(1-A^2),\quad S^{(1)}_{q_0,2\ell+1}=A(1-B^2),\quad S^{(1)}_{q_0q_0}=1-A^2B^2 ,\]

the last because \(M_{q_0p_0}=AB\) and the diagonal is \(1\). Pivoting at \(q_0\) therefore gives

\[S^{(2)}_{2k,2\ell+1} =S^{(1)}_{2k,2\ell+1}-\frac{S^{(1)}_{q_0,2k}\,S^{(1)}_{q_0,2\ell+1}}{S^{(1)}_{q_0q_0}} =B(1-A)-\frac{AB(1-A^2)(1-B^2)}{1-A^2B^2}.\]

We claim this vanishes precisely on the curve (4). Setting it to zero and clearing the denominator gives \(B(1-A)(1-A^2B^2)=AB(1-A^2)(1-B^2)\); cancelling \(B\neq0\) and then \(1-A\neq0\), and using \(1-A^2=(1-A)(1+A)\), this is equivalent to

\[1-A^2B^2=A(1+A)(1-B^2),\]

that is, to \(1=A^2B^2+A(1-B^2)+A^2(1-B^2)\). Since \(A^2B^2+A^2(1-B^2)=A^2\), the right-hand side is \(A^2+A(1-B^2)\), so the condition is exactly \(A^2+(1-B^2)A-1=0\). Thus \(S^{(2)}_{2k,2\ell+1}=0\) if and only if (4) holds: the parameters are forced onto the curve, not chosen on it, and with \(B=1/2\) the root in \((0,1)\) is \(A=(-3+\sqrt{73})/8\).

Consequently the zero set after the two forced pivots is

\[Z^{(2)}=\bigl\{\{2k,2\ell+1\}: k\neq\ell,\ k,\ell\neq k_0\bigr\},\]

in which every surviving vertex has degree exactly \(m-2\): an even survivor \(2k\) is paired with \(2\ell+1\) for each of the \(m-2\) indices \(\ell\neq k,k_0\), and symmetrically for an odd survivor. For \(m\ge3\) this degree is at least \(1\), so whichever vertex \(p_2\) is pivoted third, some pair \(\{p_2,x\}\) with \(x\) a survivor is zero, is predicted nonzero because \(G_A\) is complete, and is committed to \(L\) at that stage. Hence the third pivot fails.

Collecting the three possibilities: a first pivot may be any vertex, after which either the second pivot is not the partner \(q_0\) and fails at stage 2 by step 4, or it is \(q_0\) and the third pivot fails at stage 3 by step 5. 6. Consequences for solver authors

Two questions a reader implementing sparse Cholesky will ask deserve explicit answers. Must a solver test for these cancellations? No: symbolic factorization allocates for \(G^+_\sigma\), and by Definition 2.12 the numeric pattern is contained in the symbolic one, so a cancellation can only leave a reserved entry holding an exact zero. The factorization and the solve remain correct, at the price of a slightly over-allocated structure, and none of our counterexamples makes a standard code return a wrong answer; a solver that wanted to reclaim that space would have to test numerically during the factorization, and Theorem 5.8 shows that no ordering can guarantee those tests all fail. Is there a benefit to knowing that a clean ordering exists? Not for allocation, which is driven by \(G^+_\sigma\) either way, but for diagnosis: an exact zero at a predicted-nonzero position is an artefact of the ordering when the pattern lies in one of the classes of Theorem 4.8, whereas for \(K_6\) it may be intrinsic to the matrix (Theorem 5.8). In the statistical reading of Section 1.4 this is the difference between an unfaithfulness a reordering can remove and one it cannot. We claim no performance consequence.

7. Status and open problems

It is worth collecting in one place what the preceding sections establish and what they leave open, since a paper that pairs an impossibility result with a list of positive classes makes the two easy to conflate.

The existential question is settled, and settled negatively. Conjecture 2.26 already fails at order four: the matrix \(A^*\) of Theorem 5.1, with spectrum \(\{9,3,3,1\}\), has no ordering whose first Schur complement is cancellation-free, while \(8\) of its \(24\) orderings nevertheless deliver a clean factor. The gap between those two counts is the refill phenomenon, and isolating it is precisely what allows the stronger Conjecture 2.27 to survive at order four. Conjecture 2.27 itself fails at every even order \(n\ge6\), by the family \(M_{2m}\) of Theorem 5.8, so the smallest pattern on which cleanliness genuinely fails is the complete graph \(K_6\).

The counterexample is derived rather than exhibited. This is the part of the argument we would emphasize, because it is what distinguishes the result from a lucky search. We did not find a matrix and then verify it. The Pairing Lemma 5.5 classifies the zero-configurations available at a level with four surviving vertices, and shows that a feasible family of maximal size using all four survivors as apexes must be a fixed-point-free involution. Evenness of the order is then a consequence rather than a hypothesis; the magnitudes \(AB\) within pairs and the blocks \(\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\B&A\end{pmatrix}\) across them are consequences; and the curve \(A^2+(1-B^2)A-1=0\) is a consequence. By Remark 5.9 what emerges at each even order is a one-parameter family on a measure-zero algebraic curve, not an isolated matrix, and the inequalities \(0<B<A<1\) follow from the equation instead of being imposed alongside it. How far that rigidity extends is the subject of Question 7.1 below.

The positive side is a boundary rather than a miscellany. Conjecture 2.27 holds for every pattern of order at most four (Theorem 5.2), by an elementary case analysis that uses only the pivot update, the cancellation graph and four lemmas. Beyond that, Theorem 4.1 gives forests in the strong form that every ordering is clean, which recovers in factorization language the covariance-faithfulness theorem of Malouche and Rajaratnam [MR11], and Theorem 4.8 adds \(2\)-path strips, dominating-vertex fans, chordal graphs with exactly one triangle, and chordal graphs of clique number at most three whose triangles are pairwise edge-disjoint. The one-triangle case is adaptive of necessity: we exhibit a pattern for which no single fixed ordering is clean for every matrix of that pattern, so the quantifier order in Conjecture 2.27 is a feature of the problem and not of its phrasing. Set against the failure of \(K_6\), these classes locate the obstruction at near-completeness rather than at density or clique number alone.

The apparatus is reusable, and is the part most likely to outlast the specific results. Cleanliness is recast as a covering problem over slots: a slot \((S,\{j,k\})\) asserts \((A/A_S)_{jk}=0\), a blocking family is one that meets every ordering, and Conjecture 2.27 fails for a pattern exactly when some blocking family is realizable (Remark 2.30). The additive parametrization turns the multiplicative cancellation condition into a linear system, infeasibility of which is certified by an integer Stiemke witness, and the pattern-level lemmas — Prefix, fresh-fill closure, zero-leg, Nesting, Exchange and Monotone — are what make families refutable without reference to any particular matrix.

No theorem in this paper depends on a computation. Every result above is proved by hand, in exact arithmetic where arithmetic is needed at all. The ancillary scripts of Appendix A recompute asserted values independently, and Appendix F is explicit that both the order-four slot search and the four-vertex feasibility counts corroborate rather than constitute the proofs they accompany. The computations reported in Remark 5.7 are the one place where this reading needs care: they are assertions about cases that remain open, not steps in any argument, and nothing established above depends on them.

What is not settled. The overall shape of the results is that the positive side is governed by the scarcity of triangles and by the availability of a simplicial or universal pivot, whereas the negative side requires near-completeness. Three frontiers remain open — order five, the single \(14\)-edge pattern at order six, and all odd orders from seven upwards. The first two are finite questions that we leave undecided; the third is not, and Remark 5.7 explains why the parity argument that settles the even orders gives nothing there. To these we add the structural question of how much the even-order construction really forces.

How far order five has been pushed. The order-five question is finite, and we have gone some way into it without settling it. Using the covering reformulation of Remark 2.30 together with the pattern rules of Section 3, a depth-first search over partial blocking families closes twenty of the twenty-one connected patterns of order five: for each of them every branch ends in a refutation, by a pattern rule or, where the rules do not suffice, by an integer Stiemke certificate checked in exact arithmetic. The complete graph \(K_5\) is the exception. There the search does not terminate, with branches reaching families of some twenty slots without closing, and the natural alternative — enumerating the minimal blocking families of \(K_5\) and refuting each — is worse, since a direct enumeration passes \(2\times 10^7\) of them without exhausting the list. Because the order-five statement would rest on a single unresolved pattern, we record this as an observation rather than a theorem.

Six questions are left open: order five; the single \(14\)-edge pattern \(K_6-e\) at order six; all odd orders \(n\ge7\); general partial \(2\)-trees; outerplanar graphs in general; and whether the \(M_{2m}\) curve accounts for every even-order counterexample. Two of them we state formally: the rigidity question just mentioned, and a scheduling question which arose while proving Theorem 4.8(i) and which would extend it.

Question 7.1 (Does the curve exhaust the even-order counterexamples?).

Is every counterexample to Conjecture 2.27 at even order \(n\ge6\) a point of the \(M_{2m}\) curve (4), up to permutation and diagonal scaling?

Theorem 5.8 gives no information here, because it is existential: it produces counterexamples and shows that their parameters are forced once the pairing shape is assumed, but it never quantifies over all SPD matrices of a given order. The forcing in Lemma 5.5 is conditional in the same way. It applies to a level with exactly four survivors carrying a feasible family of maximal size that uses all four of them as apexes, and only three of the fifteen maximal feasible families at such a level meet that last condition; at five survivors the classification fails outright, since feasible families using all five apexes exist there (Remark 5.7). A counterexample resting on a different mechanism is therefore not excluded by anything we prove, and we state Question 7.1 as a question rather than as a conjecture because we have no evidence either way.

An affirmative answer would be strong, and the reason is worth recording. Every off-diagonal entry of \(M_{2m}\) is nonzero, so no point of the curve has pattern \(K_6-e\); an affirmative answer at \(n=6\) would therefore show that \(K_6-e\) admits a clean ordering for every SPD matrix of that pattern, closing one of the two finite frontiers above. Question 7.1 is in that sense at least as hard as the \(|E|=14\) case, and a counterexample to it would be at least as interesting as a proof: it would mean that the mechanism isolated by the Pairing Lemma is not the only route to failure.

Question 7.2 (Clean strategies ending at a prescribed edge).

Does every \(2\)-path strip, and every dominating-vertex fan, admit an adaptive clean strategy whose last two pivots are the two ends of a prescribed boundary edge?

An affirmative answer would give Conjecture 1 for clique sums along cliques of size at most two, and hence for strips and fans glued along an edge or a vertex. The obstruction is the final triangle. Running the strip induction from the left end leaves \(\{v_{k-2},v_{k-1},v_k\}\) as the last three survivors, and Lemma 3.2 supplies only some clean apex among them; finishing with \(K=\{v_{k-1},v_k\}\) requires the apex to be \(v_{k-2}\). If \(v_{k-2}\) is the dirty vertex of that triangle, pivoting it commits a zero at the \(K\)-pair. No refill argument repairs this, because refill (Lemma 3.5) needs the zero to pre-exist the pivot that destroys it, whereas here the offending pivot is itself the apex that creates it; and reversing the direction of the induction merely relocates the final triangle. A positive answer would also bear on the outerplanar follows from fans question above.

Sketch. Let \(G=G_1\cup G_2\) with \(G_1\cap G_2=K\) a clique, \(|K|\le2\); suppose \(G_2\) admits an adaptive clean strategy (a) eliminating \(K\) last, and \(G_1\) an adaptive clean strategy (b). Pivot \(G_2\setminus K\) by the initial segment of (a), then \(G_1\cup K\) by (b). Since no vertex of \(K\) is pivoted in phase 1, no pair meeting \(K\) is committed there, and because \(K\) separates, \(A[G_2]\) is a principal submatrix with pattern \(G_2\) on which phase 1 is exactly (a); as \(K\) comes last in (a), the pair inside \(K\) is still nonzero after phase 1. By the fill-path theorem no fill crosses the separator, so the Schur complement handed to phase 2 is \(A[G_1]\) altered only in the \(K\times K\) block and still has pattern \(G_1\); since (b) is adaptive it is valid for every SPD matrix of that pattern, so phase 2 is clean. Only hypothesis (a) is at issue, and it is exactly Question 7.2.

8. Conclusion

The existential question has a negative answer in general and a positive one on a class we can now delimit. Conjecture 2.26 is false already at order four (Theorem 5.1): the matrix \(A^*\) admits no ordering whose first Schur complement is cancellation-free, yet \(8\) of its \(24\) orderings still yield a clean factor, which is the refill phenomenon in its simplest form. Conjecture 2.27 holds for \(n\le4\) by an elementary case analysis with no computation (Theorem 5.2), and fails at every even order \(n\ge6\) through the family \(M_{2m}\) (Theorem 5.8). That family is derived rather than exhibited: classifying the zero-configurations available at a level with four surviving vertices forces a fixed-point-free involution, hence even order, and forces the magnitudes within and across pairs, so the curve \(A^2+(1-B^2)A-1=0\) on which this family lies is imposed by the classification rather than chosen. The forcing is a rigidity statement about counterexamples of this shape; it does not assert that every counterexample lies on the curve (Question 7.1). Every point of that curve with \(0<B<1\) yields a counterexample, and \(B=1/2\) is the instance we verify explicitly.

On the positive side, forests are clean in the strong sense that every ordering is clean (Theorem 4.1), and Conjecture 2.27 also holds for \(2\)-path strips, for dominating-vertex fans, for chordal graphs with exactly one triangle — adaptively, since no fixed ordering suffices — and for chordal graphs of clique number at most three whose triangles are pairwise edge-disjoint (Theorem 4.8).

Two gaps remain, and they are of different kinds. Order five we leave open: the question there is finite, pattern by pattern, but we do not settle it. Odd orders \(n\ge7\) are open for a structural reason rather than a computational one, since the pairing classification that drives the even construction requires exactly four survivors and its injectivity step fails at five (Remark 5.7); the square-count identity is evidence about where counterexamples could live rather than an obstruction to their existence, because the counterexamples we construct lie on a singular locus that such counting does not detect.

For practice the conclusion is reassuring in the direction that matters. The no-cancellation assumption of symbolic factorization remains safe for allocation, since over-allocation never threatens correctness, and none of our counterexamples causes a standard code to return a wrong answer. What is not guaranteed is the existential statement: from order six upwards there are SPD matrices for which no ordering avoids an exact zero at a position the symbolic phase predicts nonzero. The independent recomputations behind the numerical claims are reported in Appendix F and reproducible from the ancillary files listed in Appendix A.

def as_dict(M):
    n = len(M)
    return dict((i, dict((j, M[i][j]) for j in range(n))) for i in range(n))


def pivot(Mc, surv, p):
    """One elimination step: return (A/A_{S + p}) and the new survivor list."""
    rest = [v for v in surv if v != p]
    d = Mc[p][p]
    out = {}
    for j in rest:
        out[j] = dict((k, Mc[j][k] - Mc[j][p] * Mc[p][k] / d) for k in rest)
    return out, rest


def det(M):
    """Exact determinant by Gaussian elimination over Q(sqrt(D))."""
    M = [row[:] for row in M]
    n = len(M)
    d = ONE
    for c in range(n):
        p = None
        for r in range(c, n):
            if not M[r][c].is_zero():
                p = r
                break
        if p is None:
            return ZERO
        if p != c:
            M[c], M[p] = M[p], M[c]
            d = -d
        d = d * M[c][c]
        for r in range(c + 1, n):
            f = M[r][c] / M[c][c]
            if not f.is_zero():
                for k in range(c, n):
                    M[r][k] = M[r][k] - f * M[c][k]
    return d


def submatrix(M, idx):
    return [[M[i][j] for j in idx] for i in idx]

    """The pivots D_j of the unpermuted factorization; all > 0 iff SPD."""

    for _ in range(n):
        out.append(Mc[surv[0]][surv[0]])
        if len(surv) > 1:
            Mc, surv = pivot(Mc, surv, surv[0])
    return out

    for k in range(1, n + 1):
        if k == 1:
            Mk = [[ONE if i == j else ZERO for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
        else:
            prod = []
            for i in range(n):
                row = []
                for j in range(n):
                    acc = ZERO
                    for t in range(n):
                        acc = acc + M[i][t] * Mk[t][j]
                    row.append(acc)
                prod.append(row)
            for i in range(n):
                prod[i][i] = prod[i][i] + coeffs[-1]
            Mk = prod
        tr = ZERO
        for i in range(n):
            for t in range(n):
                tr = tr + M[i][t] * Mk[t][i]
        coeffs.append(-tr / k)
    return coeffs


def poly_eval(coeffs, x):
    v = ZERO
    for c in coeffs:
        v = v * x + c
    return v


def zero_pairs(Mc, surv):
    out = []
    for ii, j in enumerate(surv):
        for k in surv[ii + 1:]:
            if Mc[j][k].is_zero():
                out.append((j, k))
    return out


def show(Mc, surv, indent="      "):
    """Print a Schur complement with its surviving labels."""
    cells = {}
    for i in surv:

def clean(order, M, verbose=False):
    """Is `order` clean for M?

    An ordering is clean iff at every step every off-diagonal entry in the
    pivot's row (among survivors) is nonzero.  Those entries are exactly the
    column of L committed at that step (Definition 'factor entry'), and both
    matrices here have a COMPLETE pattern graph, so every one of them is a
    predicted-nonzero position and no separate fill-graph computation is
    needed.  Returns (is_clean, first_offence or None).
    """
    Mc, surv = as_dict(M), list(range(len(M)))
    for step, p in enumerate(order):
        if verbose:
            z = zero_pairs(Mc, surv)
            print("      stage %d: level {%s}, pivot %d; zeros among survivors: %s"
                  % (step, ", ".join(str(v) for v in order[:step]), p,
                     z if z else "none"))
            if len(M) <= 4:
                show(Mc, surv)
        for k in surv:
            if k != p and Mc[p][k].is_zero():
                if verbose:
                    print("        ^ position (%d,%d) is zero and is committed now"
                          " -> ordering is dirty" % (p, k))
                return False, (step, p, k)
        if len(surv) > 1:
            Mc, surv = pivot(Mc, surv, p)
    return True, None

"""
peo_insufficiency.py — a 2-path of 5 triangles on which no fill-free ordering is clean
Tests Remark 4.6: an SPD realization with zero clean simplicial vertices, so that a pure
PEO strategy, which never creates fill, fails.

The pattern is the 2-path strip on 0..6 (edges {i,i+1} and {i,i+2}), whose triangles are
{i,i+1,i+2}, i = 0..4. Its simplicial vertices are the ends 0 and 6. The matrix below makes
each end zero the opposite edge of its triangle: A00*A12 = A01*A02 and A66*A45 = A64*A65
(Definition 2.19), so neither end is a clean pivot at the empty level. Arithmetic is exact
(fractions), so every zero test is exact. The script checks that the matrix is SPD with this
pattern, then runs all 7! orderings through the elimination game and the Schur updates of
Definition 2.6: an ordering is clean (Definition 2.24) if every position committed to L is
nonzero, and it is a perfect elimination ordering (PEO) if it creates no fill.
"""
import itertools
from fractions import Fraction as F

N = 7
h = F(1, 2)
A = [[2, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0],
     [1, 3, h, h, 0, 0, 0],
     [1, h, 3, h, h, 0, 0],
     [0, h, h, 3, h, h, 0],
     [0, 0, h, h, 3, h, 1],
     [0, 0, 0, h, h, 3, 1],
     [0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 2]]
A = [[F(x) for x in row] for row in A]
STRIP = {frozenset((i, i + d)) for d in (1, 2) for i in range(N - d)}

def is_spd(M):
    """Symmetric, and every pivot of Gaussian elimination in natural order is positive."""
    if any(M[i][j] != M[j][i] for i in range(N) for j in range(N)):
        return False
    M = [row[:] for row in M]
    for p in range(N):
        if M[p][p] <= 0:
            return False
        for j in range(p + 1, N):
            f = M[j][p] / M[p][p]
            for k in range(p, N):
                M[j][k] -= f * M[p][k]
    return True

def eliminate(order):
    """Run `order` through the elimination game and the Schur updates.
    Returns (clean, fill_free)."""
    M = {j: {k: A[j][k] for k in range(N)} for j in range(N)}
    adj = {v: {u for u in range(N) if u != v and A[u][v] != 0} for v in range(N)}
    clean, fill_free = True, True
    for p in order:
        if any(M[p][x] == 0 for x in adj[p]):   # a committed position of L is zero
            clean = False
        nbrs = adj[p]
        for a, b in itertools.combinations(nbrs, 2):
            if b not in adj[a]:                 # the pivot creates a fill edge
                fill_free = False
                adj[a].add(b); adj[b].add(a)
        for v in nbrs:
            adj[v].discard(p)
        del adj[p]
        rest = [v for v in M if v != p]
        M = {j: {k: M[j][k] - M[j][p] * M[k][p] / M[p][p] for k in rest} for j in rest}
    return clean, fill_free

Appendix F. Independent recomputation of the numerical claims

Every numerical and combinatorial assertion of the paper has been recomputed independently, in exact arithmetic, by an independent implementation written from the statements alone (no shared code with the primary verification of Appendix A). Rational quantities were handled with exact rationals; the irrational parameter was handled by exact arithmetic in the quadratic field \(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{73})\), representing every quantity as \(p+q\sqrt{73}\) with \(p,q\in\mathbb{Q}\), so that all zero tests are exact and no floating-point tolerance is used.

The recomputation covers the spectrum and positive definiteness of \(A^*\) and of \(M_{2m}\) for \(m=2,3,4\), the two parameter identities and the vanishing of \(A^2+(1-B^2)A-1\) at \(A=(-3+\sqrt{73})/8\), the clean-ordering counts \(0/24\) and \(8/24\) for \(A^*\) and \(0/720\) and \(0/40320\) for \(M_6\) and \(M_8\), the pairing classification of Lemma 5.5, and the square-count identity \(n\lceil(n-2)/2\rceil=\binom{n}{2}\) for odd \(n\) in the range \(3\le n\le11\). Every asserted value was reproduced exactly. One quantity not asserted in the text emerged from the recomputation: there are \(15\) feasible slot sets of the maximum size \(4\) at a four-vertex level, of which only \(3\) use all four pivots, which is why Lemma 5.5 has to impose the all-four-pivots condition explicitly. The recomputation also separates the two necessary conditions of that lemma from feasibility itself: \(218\) of the \(4096\) subsets have an acyclic arc digraph, so the General Cycle Lemma 3.16 leaves \(36\) infeasible subsets undetected, and the feasible count \(182\) is a linear-programming count rather than a cycle count. None of these counts is used in the proof of Lemma 5.5, which is elementary; they are recorded here as corroboration and as context for the size bound that the lemma does prove.

It is worth recording why random search does not find the counterexamples of Section 5. Adversarial sampling on dense \(K_6\), in which the entries of a random SPD matrix are perturbed towards the cancellation conditions, bottoms out near \(296/720\) clean orderings in our runs and never approaches the \(0/720\) of \(M_6\), because \(M_6\) lies on the measure-zero curve \(A^2+(1-B^2)A-1=0\). We report this as an indication of the difficulty rather than as evidence: the perturbation scheme and the number of draws are implementation choices, and no claim in this paper depends on them. This is the coexistence noted in Section 1.4: unfaithful distributions form a null set while strong-faithfulness fails on a set of positive measure [URBY13], so the genericity heuristic and the existential failure are consistent with one another.

An exhaustive slot search at order four was also carried out, over the \(18\) slots with \(1\le|S|\le2\) on \(G_A=K_4\): of the \(2^{18}=262144\) slot families, \(69075\) are blocking and \(633\) of these are minimal, and all \(633\) are refuted by exact rational certificates (\(605\) by Stiemke's lemma, \(28\) by zero-leg propagation). This corroborates Theorem 5.2 but is no longer part of its proof, which is elementary.
